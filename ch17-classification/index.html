Why Not Just Run OLS?

Section 17.1 • Two defects, one cosmetic and one structural

The Linear Probability Model

Code the outcome 1 for recession and 0 otherwise, regress it on the spread, and read the fitted values as probabilities. This is still used, and it has two defects.

Cosmetic

A straight line does not stop at 0 or 1. Extend it far enough and it predicts negative probabilities.

Structural

The variance of a binary variable is \(p(1-p)\) — it depends on its own mean, so the errors are heteroscedastic by construction. At a typical month's \(p = 0.069\), \(p(1-p) = 0.064\); at \(p = 0.5\), 0.25: four times as large.

You can patch the second with HC3, exactly as Chapter 14 taught. You cannot patch a negative probability.

Not a Hypothetical — 84 Negative Probabilities

Fitted on the real FRED series, the linear probability model predicts values from −0.067 to 0.242.

84
months assigned a NEGATIVE probability of recession

Not a contrived example. The ordinary result of fitting a line to a variable that takes only two values.

The sigmoid cannot do this. It is impossible by construction; tuning has nothing to do with it.

The Fix: Keep the Line, Bend the Output

Keep the linear combination of features. Pass it through a function that maps the whole real line into \((0, 1)\).

$$\hat{P}(Y = 1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p)}}$$

\(e \approx 2.718\). One month, with the fitted \(\beta_0 = -1.7592\), \(\beta_1 = -0.8354\) and a spread of \(-1\) (the 10-year one point below the bill):

Step 1 · the linear predictor

\(-1.7592 + (-0.8354)(-1) = -0.9238\)

Step 2 · the exponential

\(e^{0.9238} = 2.519\)

Step 3 · bend it into (0, 1)

\(1 / (1 + 2.519) = 0.284\): a 28.4% chance

In words. The expression inside the exponential is the linear predictor — exactly the \(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}\) of Chapter 13. The sigmoid wrapped around it is S-shaped: very negative linear predictors map near 0, very positive ones near 1, and zero maps to exactly 0.5.

Nothing about the features changed. Only what we do with their weighted sum.

Linear in Log-Odds

Section 17.2 • Three scales, three interpretations, one very common error

The Rearrangement That Explains Everything

Take the sigmoid and solve for the linear predictor:

$$\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p$$

Odds \(= p/(1-p)\). A probability of 0.2 is odds of 0.25 — “one to four.”

The logit is the log of the odds. It is the scale on which this model is linear.

Logistic regression is a linear model. It is linear in the log-odds, not in the probability — and that one fact governs every interpretation you will ever make of it.

Exponentiate, and It Becomes Sayable

$$e^{\beta_j} = \text{the factor by which the odds multiply, per one-unit increase in } x_j$$ 
b = logit.params["spread"]
print(f"coefficient on spread: {b:.4f}   (p = {logit.pvalues['spread']:.3g})")
print(f"odds ratio: exp({b:.4f}) = {np.exp(b):.3f}")
coefficient on spread: -0.8354   (p = 4.88e-08)
odds ratio: exp(-0.8354) = 0.434

A curve one percentage point steeper multiplies the odds of being in recession twelve months later by 0.434 — it reduces the odds by 57%.

Check it on the fitted model: the odds are \(e^{-1.7592} = 0.1722\) at a spread of 0 and \(e^{-2.5946} = 0.0747\) at a spread of 1; \(0.0747 / 0.1722 = 0.434\). And \(1 - 0.434 = 0.566\), the 57%. (\(p = 4.88\text{e-}08\) is 0.0000000488.)

Poll — What Does −0.8354 Mean?

The fitted coefficient on the term spread is −0.8354. Which statement is correct?

A) A one-point wider spread lowers the probability of recession by 84%
B) A one-point wider spread lowers the probability of recession by 57%
C) A one-point wider spread multiplies the odds by 0.434 — a 57% fall in odds
D) A one-point wider spread lowers the log-odds by 0.434

Answer: C — the Odds, Not the Probability

A reads a log-odds coefficient as a percentage of probability — the single most common error in applied classification work. B has the right arithmetic on the wrong scale. D swaps the coefficient and its exponential: the log-odds fall by 0.8354, and it is the odds that multiply by 0.434.

So it is not a 57% reduction in probability, and it is not 84 percentage points of anything. Odds and probabilities coincide only when both are small.

This exact confusion is a standard screening question in data-science interviews. Interviewers use it because the wrong answer gets stated confidently.

The Effect on the Probability Is Not Constant

In OLS a coefficient is a constant marginal effect — one more room adds the same amount to predicted value everywhere. Here it is not:

$$\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_j} = \beta_j \, p(1-p)$$

\(\partial p / \partial x_j\): how much \(p\) moves when \(x_j\) rises by a small step.

In words. The multiplier \(p(1-p)\) is largest at \(p = 0.5\) and vanishes as \(p\) approaches 0 or 1. A given change in the spread moves the probability a great deal for a month that is truly uncertain, and hardly at all for one that is already safe. At a spread of \(-2\), \(p = 0.478\): \(-0.8354 \times 0.478 \times 0.522 = -0.208\). At \(+2\), \(p = 0.031\): \(-0.8354 \times 0.031 \times 0.969 = -0.025\), about 8 times smaller.

That is economically sensible, not a mathematical inconvenience. If a recession is already near-certain, more bad news barely changes the forecast. The model's diminishing sensitivity at the extremes matches how information actually works.

Fitting, and Reading the Output

Section 17.3 • No closed form, two libraries, and a variable that adds nothing

There Is No Formula — and It Barely Matters

OLS has a closed form. Logistic regression does not: the estimate is found numerically, by maximum likelihood.

$$L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{y_i}(1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}$$

In words. Each month contributes \(p_i\) if a recession followed and \(1 - p_i\) if none did. Maximising this picks the coefficients under which the data we actually saw is most probable.

At most one optimum. The log-likelihood is concave (a single hill), so if a maximum exists it is the only one: no local maxima to get trapped in, and two analysts get the same answer to many decimals.

But it can fail to converge — and failure is diagnostic: a feature that separates the classes perfectly, wildly different scales, or near-collinearity. Chapter 14's problem, inherited.

A convergence warning is information about your design matrix, not a nuisance to suppress.

Two Libraries, Two Purposes — and a Catch

# Inference — standard errors, z-statistics (a large-sample t), p-values
X = sm.add_constant(df[['spread']])     # statsmodels needs the constant
logit = sm.Logit(y, X).fit()

# Prediction — the scikit-learn pipeline ecosystem (adds its own intercept)
clf = LogisticRegression(C=np.inf, tol=1e-8).fit(df[['spread']], y)  # C=np.inf: no penalty

Turning the penalty off with C=np.inf is not optional if you want to match statsmodels. scikit-learn applies \(L_2\) regularization by default. (Before version 1.8: penalty=None.)

Sensible for prediction. Surprising for an economist who expects maximum likelihood (the coefficients that make the observed outcomes most probable, Section 3) and gets a penalised estimate without being told. And worse: the strength parameter is C, which is the inverse of the penalty — small C means heavy regularization. Chapter 16 showed “alpha” meaning penalty strength in scikit-learn and the L1/L2 mix in glmnet; C is one more convention, and it runs the opposite way: it is the inverse of the strength.

Why Accuracy Is the Wrong Score

Section 17.4 • The chapter. The benchmark is the base rate.

The Reason Is Arithmetic

pred = (p_hat >= 0.5).astype(int)
print(f"'always no recession':    accuracy {(y == 0).mean():.1%}")
print(f"logistic at p >= 0.5:     accuracy {(pred == y).mean():.1%}")
print(f"recession months caught:  {(pred[y == 1] == 1).sum()} of {(y == 1).sum()}")
'always no recession':    accuracy 93.1%
logistic at p >= 0.5:     accuracy 93.1%
recession months caught:  0 of 36

Only 6.9% of months precede a recession, so a model that says “no” every time is right 93.1% of the time for free.

Accuracy's benchmark is set by the base rate — and with imbalanced classes that benchmark is high enough to disguise total uselessness.

And 0.5 is a threshold nobody chose. It is a library default, inherited from a two-class problem with a 50% base rate. With a 6.9% base rate the predicted probability rarely climbs above 0.5, so the rule almost never fires.

And the Rarer the Event, the Worse It Gets

Prediction is valuable precisely when an event is rare and consequential — which is precisely when accuracy stops working. Accuracy is least informative exactly where classification matters most.

Scored Two Ways

By accuracy, a tie: a rule with no inputs at all matches our yield-curve logit.

By the Brier score (the average squared gap between each predicted probability and what happened, 1 or 0; lower is better), the logistic model beats both the do-nothing rule and a model that just reports the base rate every month.

The information was there the whole time. Accuracy threw it away, by collapsing a well-ordered set of probabilities into a yes/no call at an arbitrary cutoff.

ROC-AUC 0.811: given one month that preceded a recession and one that did not, the model ranks them correctly about 81% of the time.

Accuracy is \((TP + TN)/n\) — it adds the two diagonal cells and divides. With a 6.9% base rate the true negatives dominate that sum, so accuracy is almost entirely a measure of how well you identify quiet months. 487 out of 523 is 93.1%.

Recall \(= TP/(TP+FN)\): of the recession months, the share flagged (here 0 of 36). Precision \(= TP/(TP+FP)\): of the alarms, the share that were real (here there are no alarms).

Any single number computed from this table discards information. Accuracy discards the part you care about.

Accuracy Is an Improper Score

A scoring rule is strictly proper if it is uniquely optimised by reporting your true beliefs. Brier and log-loss (the average of \(-\log\) of the probability the model gave to what actually happened) are strictly proper. Accuracy is not — it can be improved by misreporting: shading a real 0.45 up to a confident 1 pays off whenever the outcome occurs, and accuracy cannot tell a well-calibrated 0.45 from an overconfident 0.95.

$$\text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\hat{p}_i - y_i\right)^2$$

In words. Squared error, applied to probabilities. It rewards being both discriminating (separating the classes) and calibrated (when you say 30%, it happens 30% of the time). Accuracy rewards only being on the right side of a cutoff.

Score classifiers with Brier or log-loss. Report accuracy as a summary for stakeholders, never as an objective to optimise — and never without the base rate beside it.

A Precise Footnote to Chapter 15

Chapter 15 gave you a limit: \(\sigma^2\), the irreducible error, which no model could beat. Classification has a benchmark, which is a weaker thing.

A model that reports 6.9% every month and never looks at the spread scores \(\bar{p}(1-\bar{p}) = \mathbf{0.0641}\) on the Brier score. Our logistic fit, at 0.0615, beats it. The true limit, the Brier score of the true probabilities, is unknown and lower still.

Be careful with this. That is a statement about squared error applied to probabilities, which is where the argument is legitimate. It is not a bias–variance decomposition of classification loss: the clean decomposition is squared-error only, and there is no tidy analogue for 0/1 loss.

Any procedure that tunes a threshold to maximise accuracy on imbalanced data will drive you back to the top row and a model that never fires.

And It Is Just Arithmetic, Once You Name the Costs

If a false negative costs \(C_{FN}\) and a false positive costs \(C_{FP}\), expected cost is minimised at

$$t^* = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}}$$

In words. The optimal threshold depends only on the ratio of the costs — not on their level, not on the sample size, not on the model. If missing a recession is nine times worse than a false alarm, \(t^* = 0.1\): the bottom row of the table, derived rather than guessed.

Lending. A default costs most of the principal. A declined good applicant costs that loan's margin, which is much smaller. Lab 17 prices them at $12,000 and $1,600 (7.5:1, \(t^* = 0.118\)); thinner margins push the ratio far higher.

Criminal justice. A false positive means detaining someone who would not have reoffended. The ratio is contested, not obvious — a political question presented as a technical one.

Think Like an Economist

Before choosing a threshold, write down what each error costs — in dollars, or in whatever the decision actually trades.

Most classification work skips this and inherits 0.5 from a library, which, without saying so, asserts that false positives and false negatives cost exactly the same. That assertion is almost never true, and it is one an economist is unusually well equipped to interrogate.

The cost ratio is the modelling assumption. The threshold is just its arithmetic consequence.

Someone chooses the threshold. If the analyst does not do it explicitly, the library has already done it for them.

Poll — What Went Wrong?

From January 2023 to September 2024 the fitted probability of recession ran at 25–42%, and no recession came. What went wrong?

A) It overfitted the 1980s and 1990s
B) Temporal leakage — it was fitted on data including its own future

Answer: D — the Model Was Not Broken

A, B and C are the three tools of the last three chapters — and none of them is the answer. Every diagnostic in Chapter 14 would pass. The likelihood was correctly maximised, the coefficient correctly estimated, the probabilities correctly computed. B is a real flaw here, but it pulled the numbers down: fitted on data up to 2021, the same months come out at 76–89%.

This is a structural break in the relationship between a feature and the outcome. No amount of better classification technique detects it, and finding it requires knowing what a term premium is.

What Actually Happened

A ten-year yield decomposes into expected future short rates plus a term premium — the extra compensation investors demand for holding duration risk (a long bond loses value when rates rise).

Years of quantitative easing (the Fed buying long-term bonds) had pushed that premium to around zero or below (New York Fed, Adrian–Crump–Moench estimates).

The curve inverted partly because the term premium had been squeezed toward zero, not because markets expected a contraction. The spread was numerically the same. What it indicated was not.

It is not overfitting, leakage, miscalibration or a bad threshold — the four failures this chapter and the last two have taught you to look for.

A model is a claim that a historical relationship will continue. Deciding whether it will is a question about the economy, not about the model.

How You Would Actually Catch It

Undetectable from performance metrics — but not undetectable. Three habits, and all three are economics rather than statistics.

1

Decompose the feature. The spread is expected short rates plus a term premium, and they move independently. Any composite indicator has this property, and watching the components reveals when the composite is being driven by an unusual part.

2

Ask what mechanism the model encodes. “Inverted curve predicts recession” is a correlation. The mechanism is that markets expecting a downturn bid up long bonds in anticipation of rate cuts. Written that way, the question becomes testable.

3

Track the regime, not just the residuals. A model's errors look fine until suddenly they do not, and by then you have been wrong for two years. Ask whether the conditions that made the feature work still hold — you can check that continuously.

None of these is a metric you can compute from the fitted object. They require knowing why the model works.

Economist at Work — The NY Fed's Model

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York has published a term-spread recession probability for decades. It is deliberately parsimonious — one variable, a probit link (a close cousin of our logit) — from Estrella and Mishkin's work in the 1990s, with parameters estimated on 1959–2009.

Parsimony is the point. A model with one interpretable input, published monthly for thirty years, is auditable in a way a hundred-feature ensemble never is.

From mid-2022 it began flashing, and forecasters who took it at face value called for a downturn through 2023 and into 2024. It did not come. The Fed has not discarded the model, and neither should you.

The lesson: a feature's predictive content is a property of the monetary regime that produced it, and regimes change. Asking periodically whether an input still means what it meant is the maintenance task that separates a model in production from a model in a paper.

Beyond Two Categories

Many economic questions are not binary: which of four transport modes a commuter chooses, which of five ratings a bond receives, whether a firm expands, contracts or holds steady.

$$P(Y = j \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{e^{\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j}}{\sum_{m=1}^{J} e^{\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}_m}}$$

In words. Multinomial logistic regression fits one linear predictor per category and normalises them into probabilities. One category is fixed as the baseline and the rest are read relative to it — Chapter 13's reference-category logic, moved from a predictor to the outcome. This family of models comes from McFadden's work on discrete choice, which won the 2000 Nobel Prize.

Its famous weakness. The red bus / blue bus problem: introduce an identical blue bus and the model cuts car use from a half to a third, because it cannot know the two buses are near-substitutes; in reality the buses just split the bus riders.

For ordered outcomes — bond ratings, survey scales — ordered logit exploits the ordering with one coefficient vector and a set of cutpoints. Usually the right choice.

A Workflow, and What Comes Next

Section 17.7 • Six steps, in this order

A Classification Workflow

1

Check the base rate before anything else. One line, and it determines how you must evaluate everything downstream.

2

Fit on the true distribution. Resampling (copying or dropping rows to even out the classes) and class_weight='balanced' both move the prior and inflate every predicted probability. If the probability is the product, fit unweighted and move the threshold instead.

3

Score with a proper rule (one that rewards honest probabilities). Brier or log-loss, beside a discrimination measure such as AUC. Report accuracy only next to the base rate.

4

Choose the threshold from costs. \(t^* = C_{FP}/(C_{FP}+C_{FN})\). The arithmetic is trivial; naming the costs is the hard part and it is not a statistical task.

5

Check calibration (when it says 30%, it happens about 30% of the time) if the probability is used as a number — if it feeds an expected-loss or capital formula, being well-ordered is not enough.

6

Ask whether the features still mean what they meant. Schedule the question rather than waiting for the model to embarrass you.

Key Takeaways

1. The linear probability model produces impossible predictions — 84 negative probabilities on real FRED data — and heteroscedastic errors by construction. The logit link fixes both by modelling log-odds linearly.

2. A logistic coefficient is a change in log-odds: −0.8354 means the odds multiply by 0.434, a 57% reduction in odds, and the effect on the probability depends on where you start.

3. Accuracy is an improper score (it can be raised by reporting overconfident probabilities), and under imbalance a model with no information already scores 1 minus the base rate (93.1%). Ours matched it while flagging 0 of the 36 months that preceded a recession — yet Brier 0.0615 and AUC 0.811 show the information is there.

4. 0.5 is a library default, not a decision. At 0.10 the model flags 26 of the 36 recession months at the worst accuracy in the table, and the right cutoff is \(C_{FP}/(C_{FP}+C_{FN})\) — a question about costs.

5. A model can be mathematically sound and still fail if a feature stops meaning what it meant — two years with the 10-year yield below the 3-month bill, in-sample probabilities of 25–42%, no recession, because QE had compressed the term premium.
