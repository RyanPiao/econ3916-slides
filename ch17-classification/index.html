Section 17.1 • Two defects, one cosmetic and one structural

The Linear Probability Model

The model we know: code a recession ahead 1, none 0, regress on the spread, read the fit as a probability.

The fitted line (OLS on all 523 months)

\(\hat{p} = 0.1511 - 0.0527 \times \text{spread}\)

Defect 1 · Cosmetic

March 2010: \(0.1511 - 0.0527 \times 3.58 \approx -0.04\), a negative probability. The line crosses 0 at a spread of 2.87; 84 months lie beyond.

Defect 2 · Structural

A 0/1 outcome has variance \(p(1-p)\), so the errors are heteroscedastic by construction: 0.064 at \(p = 0.069\), 0.25 at \(p = 0.5\), four times as large.

HC3 patches Defect 2 (Chapter 14). Nothing patches a negative probability.

Not a Hypothetical — 84 Negative Probabilities

Every dot is a month: the quiet ones at 0, the 36 followed by a recession at 1.

The straight line runs from 0.242 down to −0.067. Every month with a spread above 2.87 gets a negative probability:

84
months with a NEGATIVE probability of recession

The sigmoid cannot do this. At the widest spread, 4.15 in September 1982, the line says −0.067 and the sigmoid says 0.005: small, and still a probability.

The Fix: Keep the Line, Bend the Output

Keep the weighted sum of features; pass it through a function that maps any number into \((0, 1)\).

$$\hat{P}(Y = 1 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p)}}$$

\(e \approx 2.718\). One month: fitted \(\beta_0 = -1.7592\), \(\beta_1 = -0.8354\), spread \(-1\) (10-year one point below the bill):

Step 1 · the linear predictor

\(-1.7592 + (-0.8354)(-1) = -0.9238\)

Step 2 · the exponential

\(e^{0.9238} = 2.519\)

Step 3 · bend it into (0, 1)

\(1 / (1 + 2.519) = 0.284\): a 28.4% chance

In words. \(z\), the linear predictor, is Chapter 13's \(\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}\). The sigmoid (the S) turns any \(z\) into a probability: very negative near 0, very positive near 1, \(z = 0\) exactly 0.5. The features did not change; only what we do with their sum.

Linear in Log-Odds

Section 17.2 • Three scales, three interpretations, one very common error

The Rearrangement That Explains Everything

1 Log-odds · −0.8354 per point (linear)
2 Odds · ×0.434 per point
3 Probability · depends where you start

Take the sigmoid and solve for the linear predictor:

$$\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p$$

Probability \(p\): out of every outcome. Odds \(= p/(1-p)\): chances for against chances against. Logit: the log of the odds.

Toss a coin: heads

Probability \(\tfrac12\): one head in two tosses.

Odds \(0.5/0.5 = 1\), 1 to 1 (“even”): a head for every tail.

Log-odds \(\log 1 = 0\).

Rock-paper-scissors: you win

Probability \(\tfrac13\): win, lose, tie equally likely.

Odds \((\tfrac13)/(\tfrac23) = 0.5\), 1 to 2: one win per two non-wins.

Log-odds \(\log 0.5 = -0.69\).

Our data: a recession ahead

Probability \(36/523 = 0.069\).

Odds \(36/487 = 0.074\), about 1 to 13.5.

Log-odds \(-2.60\).

Probability: 0 to 1. Odds: 0 to infinity, even = 1. Log-odds: no floor, no ceiling, even = 0, so a straight line fits. That is why the model is linear in the log-odds.

The Effect on the Probability Is Not Constant

1 Log-odds · −0.8354 per point (linear)
2 Odds · ×0.434 per point
3 Probability · depends where you start

The question people ask: how many points does the probability move? It depends on where it starts. (In OLS, the same everywhere.)

$$\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_j} = \beta_j \, p(1-p)$$

\(\partial p / \partial x_j\): how much \(p\) moves when \(x_j\) rises by a small step.

The multiplier \(p(1-p)\) peaks at \(p = 0.5\) and shrinks toward 0 near 0 or 1.

An uncertain month: spread −2, \(p = 0.478\)

\(-0.8354 \times 0.478 \times 0.522 = -0.208\): about 21 points per point of spread.

A safe month: spread +2, \(p = 0.031\)

\(-0.8354 \times 0.031 \times 0.969 = -0.025\): about 2.5 points per point of spread.

About 8 times smaller. A month already near-certain barely moves on one more piece of news.

Fitting, and Reading the Output

Section 17.3 • No closed form, two libraries, and a variable that adds nothing

There Is No Formula — and It Barely Matters

OLS has a formula (Topic 13: \(\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\)). Logistic regression has none: maximum likelihood tries coefficients and keeps those that make the months we saw most probable.

$$L(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_i^{\,y_i}(1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}$$

\(p_i\): the model’s probability for month \(i\) · \(y_i\): 1 if a recession followed, else 0 · \(\prod\): multiply over all 523 months. Each month adds \(p_i\) or \(1 - p_i\).

Count it. 523 months; a recession followed in 36, so the base rate, the share of “yes” before any evidence, is 36 / 523 = 6.9%.

“No” every month is right 487 times: 487 / 523 = 93.1%, which is 1 − 6.9%.

Accuracy’s benchmark is 1 − the base rate, high enough at 6.9% to hide a model that does nothing. Topic 5’s lesson again: a 99%-accurate fraud test was right 4.7% of the times it fired, because fraud’s base rate is 0.1%.

And 0.5 is a threshold nobody chose, a library default. Our model’s highest probability in 523 months is 0.423 (May 2023), so the 0.5 rule fires in 0 months.

Accuracy = all the right calls / all months = (TP + TN) / n = (0 + 487) / 523 = 93.1%. The 487 quiet months do all the work.

Recall = recession months caught / all recession months = TP / (TP + FN) = 0 / 36 = 0%. Topic 5 called it sensitivity.

Precision = real alarms / all alarms = TP / (TP + FP) = 0 / 0: no alarms, so there is nothing to score. (Topic 5’s fraud test: 4.7%.)

Accuracy 93.1%, recall 0%. Accuracy hides the part you care about.

Accuracy Is an Improper Score

What makes a score reward honest probabilities?

A score is strictly proper if only your true beliefs get the best score on average. Brier and log-loss (the average −log of the probability given to what happened) are. Accuracy is not.

Example. A month whose true chance of a recession is 70%. Two forecasters:

ReportsAccuracy (cutoff 0.5)Brier on average: 0.7 × (r − 1)² + 0.3 × r²
0.70, honestsays yes: right 70%0.7 × 0.09 + 0.3 × 0.49 = 0.21
1.00, overconfidentsays yes: right 70%0.7 × 0 + 0.3 × 1 = 0.30

Accuracy cannot tell them apart. Brier rewards the honest one: 0.21 against 0.30 (lower is better). Our own case: “always no” is the model rounded to 0, the same 93.1%, and a worse Brier (0.0688 against 0.0615).

So Brier rewards separating the classes and being calibrated (say 30%, it happens 30% of the time): \(\text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n}\sum (\hat p_i - y_i)^2\).

Score with Brier or log-loss. Report accuracy only as a summary, and always beside the base rate.

Is there a best possible Brier score, like Chapter 15’s limit?

Chapter 15: no model beats \(\sigma^2\), the irreducible error. Classification has only a benchmark: the score for reporting the base rate, 6.9%, every month.

A recession month costs \((0.069 - 1)^2 = 0.867\). A quiet month costs \(0.069^2 = 0.0047\).
Average over 36 and 487 months: \(\bar p(1 - \bar p) = 0.069 \times 0.931 = \mathbf{0.0641}\).

And It Is Just Arithmetic, Once You Name the Costs

If a false negative costs \(C_{FN}\) and a false positive costs \(C_{FP}\), expected cost is minimised at

$$t^* = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}}$$

In words. Only the ratio of the costs matters, not their level, the sample size or the model. A miss nine times worse than an alarm gives \(t^* = 0.1\): the table's bottom row, derived, not guessed.

Lending. A default costs most of the principal; a declined good applicant, only the margin. Lab 17: $12,000 against $1,600 (7.5:1, \(t^* = 0.118\)).

Criminal justice. A false positive detains someone who would not have reoffended. The ratio is contested: a political question dressed as a technical one.

Think Like an Economist

Before choosing a threshold, write down what each error costs — in dollars, or in whatever the decision actually trades.

Most classification work skips this and inherits 0.5 from a library, which, without saying so, asserts that false positives and false negatives cost exactly the same. That assertion is almost never true, and it is one an economist is unusually well equipped to interrogate.

The cost ratio is the modelling assumption. The threshold is just its arithmetic consequence.

Someone chooses the threshold. If the analyst does not do it explicitly, the library has already done it for them.

Answer: D — the Model Was Not Broken

A, B and C are the three tools of the last three chapters — and none of them is the answer. Every diagnostic in Chapter 14 would pass. The likelihood was correctly maximised, the coefficient correctly estimated, the probabilities correctly computed. B is a real flaw here, but it pulled the numbers down: fitted on data up to 2021, the same months come out at 76–89%.

This is a structural break in the relationship between a feature and the outcome. No amount of better classification technique detects it, and finding it requires knowing what a term premium is.

What Actually Happened

A ten-year yield = expected future short rates + a term premium, the extra pay for holding a long bond (which loses value when rates rise).

Years of quantitative easing (the Fed buying long bonds) pushed that premium to around zero or below (New York Fed, Adrian–Crump–Moench).

The curve inverted partly because the term premium was squeezed toward zero, not because markets expected a contraction. Same number; it meant something else.

Not overfitting, leakage, miscalibration or a bad threshold: none of the four failures we have learned to check.

A model is a claim that a historical relationship will continue. Deciding whether it will is a question about the economy, not about the model.

How You Would Actually Catch It

Invisible to metrics, but not undetectable. Three habits, all economics, not statistics.

1

Decompose the feature. The spread is expected short rates plus a term premium, moving independently. Watch the parts to see when an unusual one drives the total.

2

Name the mechanism. “Inverted curve predicts recession” is a correlation. The mechanism: markets expecting a downturn bid up long bonds ahead of rate cuts. Written that way, it is testable.

3

Track the regime, not just the residuals. Errors look fine until they do not, and by then you have been wrong for two years. Keep checking that the conditions behind the feature still hold.

None comes from the fitted object. Each needs knowing why the model works.

Economist at Work — The NY Fed's Model

The New York Fed has published a term-spread recession probability for decades: deliberately parsimonious, one variable, a probit link (a close cousin of our logit), from Estrella and Mishkin (1990s), estimated on 1959–2009.

Parsimony is the point: one interpretable input is auditable in a way a hundred-feature ensemble never is.

From mid-2022 it flashed; forecasters who took it at face value called a downturn for 2023–24. None came. The Fed kept the model, and so should you.

A feature's predictive content belongs to the monetary regime that produced it, and regimes change. Asking whether an input still means what it meant is the maintenance a production model needs.

Beyond Two Categories

Many questions are not binary: a commuter's transport mode, a bond's rating, whether a firm expands, contracts or holds.

$$P(Y = j \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{e^{\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}_j}}{\sum_{m=1}^{J} e^{\mathbf{x}'\boldsymbol{\beta}_m}}$$

In words. Multinomial logistic regression: one linear predictor per category, normalised into probabilities, read against a baseline category (Chapter 13's reference category, now on the outcome). From McFadden's discrete-choice work, Nobel Prize 2000.

Its famous weakness: red bus / blue bus. Add an identical blue bus and the model cuts car use from a half to a third; in reality the buses just split the bus riders.

Ordered outcomes (ratings, survey scales): ordered logit, one coefficient vector plus cutpoints. Usually the right choice.

A Classification Workflow

1

Check the base rate first. One line; it sets how you evaluate everything after.

2

Fit on the true distribution. Resampling (copying or dropping rows to even the classes) and class_weight='balanced' inflate every probability. If the probability is the product, fit unweighted and move the threshold.

3

Score with a proper rule (rewards honest probabilities): Brier or log-loss, beside AUC. Accuracy only next to the base rate.

4

Choose the threshold from costs. \(t^* = C_{FP}/(C_{FP}+C_{FN})\). Naming the costs is the hard part, and not a statistical one.

5

Check calibration (says 30%, happens 30%) if the probability is used as a number, as in an expected-loss formula.

6

Ask whether the features still mean what they meant. Put it on the calendar.

Key Takeaways

1. The linear probability model gives 84 negative probabilities on real FRED data and heteroscedastic errors. The logit fixes both: linear in log-odds.

2. A coefficient is a change in log-odds: −0.8354 multiplies the odds by 0.434 (−57% in odds); the probability effect depends on where you start.

3. Accuracy is an improper score, and under imbalance doing nothing scores 93.1%. Ours matched it, flagging 0 of 36; Brier 0.0615 and AUC 0.811 show the information is there.

4. 0.5 is a library default, not a decision. At 0.10 it flags 26 of 36 at the worst accuracy; the right cutoff is \(C_{FP}/(C_{FP}+C_{FN})\), a question about costs.

5. A sound model fails when a feature stops meaning what it meant: two years inverted, in-sample probabilities of 25–42%, no recession, because QE had compressed the term premium.
