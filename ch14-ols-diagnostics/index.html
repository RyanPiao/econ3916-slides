A. What a Standard Error Assumes

Ten minutes • §14.0–14.1

2. Two scorecards

4. Frisch–Waugh–Lovell

What “controlling for” actually does. Next slide.

Frisch–Waugh–Lovell: What “Controlling For” Means

The coefficient on \(x_j\) in a multivariate regression is identical to the coefficient from a three-step procedure: residualise \(y\) against the other predictors, residualise \(x_j\) against the same others, then regress the first residuals on the second.

$$\hat{\beta}_j = \frac{\text{Cov}(\tilde{y}, \tilde{x}_j)}{\text{Var}(\tilde{x}_j)}$$

What this says in words: “holding constant” is a literal operation: strip out of both sides everything the other variables can explain, and regress what is left on what is left. Everything else is discarded before the coefficient is computed.

Remember this one. Chapter 24 defines Double Machine Learning as exactly this procedure with the two first stages replaced by ML models.

Key Concept

A standard error is a claim about residuals.

Every standard error OLS prints comes from a formula, and that formula assumes things about the errors: that they all have the same variance, and that they are unrelated to one another.

Residuals are observable. So unlike the causal assumptions of Chapters 10–13, these are directly testable — and when they fail, the failure is in the standard error, not the coefficient.

The Picture Before the Algebra

The line is equally good in both. But on the right, the observations at the right-hand end carry far less information than those on the left — and a formula that treats every observation as equally informative will overstate how much you know.

Spherical Errors: One Equation, Two Claims

$$E[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}' \mid \mathbf{X}] = \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}_n$$

The diagonal is constant

Every observation has the same error variance. Homoscedasticity.

§B breaks this one.

The off-diagonals are zero

No error is related to any other. No autocorrelation.

§C breaks this one.

What this says in words: the Gauss–Markov theorem makes OLS the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator, but it makes two separate promises in one line. Today we break them one at a time.

The Sandwich — Named, Not Derived

WHEN THE ASSUMPTION HOLDS

$$\widehat{\text{Var}}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}}) = \hat{\sigma}^2 (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}$$

WHAT IS ACTUALLY TRUE

$$(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}(\mathbf{X}'\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{X})(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}$$

What this says in words: the two outer terms are the bread; the middle is the filling; \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) is the true error covariance matrix, whatever it happens to be. Set \(\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}\) and the sandwich collapses back to the classical formula.

So the classical estimator is a special case, not a different thing — and every robust standard error you will ever use is a different guess at the filling. You are not asked to derive it. You are asked to know it is one guess among several, and usually the wrong one.

Common Misconception

“Heteroscedasticity biases my coefficients.”

It does not. OLS stays unbiased and consistent — the coefficient is still, on average, right. What breaks is the variance of the estimator, and therefore every standard error, \(t\)-statistic, \(p\)-value and confidence interval computed from it.

Predicting? This may be nearly irrelevant to you. (Hold that thought — Ch 15.)

Testing a hypothesis? It can be the difference between a finding and nothing.

Confusing this with omitted variable bias — which does corrupt the coefficient — is the most common error on this topic in interviews.

B. Heteroscedasticity in Practice

Twelve minutes • §14.2

Two Tests, Both Regressions on Squared Residuals

Breusch–Pagan

Regress squared residuals on the predictors. Do they explain anything?

White's test

Same, plus squares and cross-products — catches non-linear variance patterns BP misses.

Breusch-Pagan: LM = 635.0    p = 2.55e-137
White test:    LM = 1,971.6   p = 0

But do not over-invest in the tests. With \(n = 20{,}640\) almost any test rejects; with \(n = 30\) almost none do. The significance of a diagnostic is mostly a statement about sample size. Plot the residuals and look — Chapter 3's lesson, one level up.

The Plot That Catches More Than Every Test Combined

Two Findings in One Table

residual SD:    0.712  0.687  0.826  0.875  0.890
residual mean: +0.017 -0.087 -0.051 -0.012 +0.133

The spread rises 25%

Cheapest to most expensive block groups. That is the heteroscedasticity the tests flagged.

The means are not flat

Negative in the middle, positive at both ends. An arch means functional form, not variance. §F returns to this.

What this costs a decision-maker. One RMSE for the whole model averages a residual SD of 0.712 with one of 0.890. If the expensive neighbourhoods are the ones the model gets used on, the headline accuracy is optimistic by roughly a quarter. Report error by segment.

Keep OLS, Change the Variance Formula

Stop using a variance formula you know to be wrong.

$$\widehat{\text{Var}}_{HC}(\boldsymbol{\hat{\beta}}) = (\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i e_i^2 \mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i'\right)(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}$$

What this says in words: same bread, new filling — built from each observation's own squared residual \(e_i^2\) instead of one common \(\sigma^2\). HC0 sets \(w_i = 1\). HC3 sets \(w_i = 1/(1-h_{ii})^2\), where \(h_{ii}\) is observation \(i\)'s leverage.

Why the leverage correction: high-leverage points pull the fitted line toward themselves, which artificially shrinks their own residuals and flatters the model. HC3 inflates those residuals back up in proportion to how much pulling they did.

Use HC3 by default. It costs one argument: model.fit(cov_type='HC3')

Answer: C — Robust Errors Are Not a Uniform Tax

Every coefficient identical. Every standard error larger. They reprice each coefficient according to how much of its apparent precision came from the homoscedasticity assumption — and AveRooms was living almost entirely on credit.

C. Dependence, and Why You Cluster

Fifteen minutes • §14.3 • the most consequential thing today

Your Sample Is Smaller Than Its Row Count

The second half of the spherical-errors assumption says no two errors are related. In economic data they routinely are:

students within a school

workers within a firm

households within a neighbourhood

quarters within a country

A thousand people in one town who all lived through the same local recession are not a thousand independent observations about recessions.

They are closer to one.

A Policy Assigned at the Region Level

40 regions × 250 people = 10,000 rows. Treatment assigned per region. True effect exactly zero.

naive SE     0.0260   t = 0.94
clustered SE 0.2613   t = 0.09
inflation: 10.1x

A ten-fold difference in claimed precision from the same 10,000 rows.

On this one draw neither is significant, so the room shrugs. Good — that is the setup.

Poll 3 — Predict It

Now run that experiment 2,000 times. The true effect is zero every time and the test is set at the 5% level. How often do the naive standard errors declare a significant effect?

A   About 5% — that is what the level means
B   About 20%
C   About 50%
D   About 85%

Answer: D — Eighty-Five Percent

Common Misconception

“My sample is huge, so I don't need robust standard errors.”

Sample size is the reason to worry, not the reason not to. The 85% happened because the naive formula treated 10,000 correlated observations as 10,000 independent ones — and the more rows you add inside the same 40 regions, the more overconfident it becomes.

What matters is the number of clusters, not the number of rows.

Growing \(n\) inside a fixed number of clusters gains almost nothing and costs you calibration.

When to Cluster — the Rule That Replaced the Old One

The old answer was “cluster if you sampled in groups” — which never explained why researchers cluster by state but not by gender.

Abadie, Athey, Imbens & Wooldridge (2023) reframed it: clustering is a question about experimental design, not sampling. Cluster when the sampling drew clusters from a broader population, or — the condition that decides most real cases — the treatment was assigned at the cluster level.

A pricing change randomised per user?

No geographic clustering needed — the assignment mechanism has no spatial structure.

A policy that switches on per state?

Cluster by state — however many individuals you observe.

The unit of assignment, not the unit of observation, sets the level.

Key Concept

Cluster at the level of treatment assignment.

Do not ask “are my observations correlated?” — they always are, somehow. Ask “at what level was the treatment assigned?”

Forty regions is forty pieces of information about a region-level policy, whether you observe 10,000 people or 10 million.

Think Like an Economist

Before choosing a clustering level, describe the mechanism that assigned your treatment, in a sentence, out loud.

“The state legislature passed it, so every resident of that state got it on the same day.”

That answers the question — cluster by state. If you cannot describe the mechanism, you do not yet know enough about your data to report a standard error from it, and no amount of robustness options will substitute.

D. A Diagnostic That Answers the Wrong Question

Six minutes • §14.3b • a small mystery

Serial Correlation — and a Result That Cannot Be

Durbin–Watson

0 to 4, with 2 meaning no first-order correlation. Tests only AR(1); invalid with a lagged dependent variable.

Breusch–Godfrey

The modern replacement. Arbitrary lag order, valid with lagged dependent variables. Use it.

Now run both on our California Housing regression — cross-sectional data with no time dimension at all:

Durbin-Watson    0.805
Breusch-Godfrey  LM = 9,861.3   p = 0

Strong, overwhelmingly significant serial correlation — in data with no time ordering whatsoever.

Poll 4

Durbin–Watson is 0.805 on cross-sectional data. What is the test actually detecting?

A   Nothing — the test is broken on cross-sectional data
B   Geography — the rows are ordered by location, so adjacent rows are adjacent places
C   Real serial correlation — the data has a hidden time dimension
D   Heteroscedasticity leaking into an autocorrelation statistic

Answer: B — Geography in a Serial-Correlation Costume

Shuffle the rows and run identical code:

Durbin-Watson    1.973
Breusch-Godfrey  LM = 7.5   p = 0.057

It vanished. Same data, same model, same coefficients — only the row order changed, and a \(p\)-value of zero became 0.057. California Housing is ordered geographically, so what the tests detected was real spatial dependence.

Two Lessons, and the Second Is the General One

Practically: do not run serial-correlation tests on cross-sectional data unless the row order means something — and if it does, say what it means.

Generally: a diagnostic answers the question it was built to answer, not the question you had in mind. Both tests behaved perfectly. The mismatch was ours.

Common Misconception: “a significant diagnostic test means my model is broken.”

It means an assumption is violated. Which one, whether it matters, and what to do are three further questions the test does not answer. Read a test as evidence to interpret, not a rule to obey.

E. Leverage and Influence

Twelve minutes • §14.4 • how little data is your finding standing on?

Three Words, Three Different Things

Extremity alone is not a problem. Extremity plus disagreement is. A block group with 140 rooms per household is wildly extreme in \(x\), but if its value falls right on the fitted line, deleting it changes almost nothing.

Measuring It

Leverage is \(h_{ii}\), the \(i\)th diagonal of \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\) — the same quantity HC3 uses. It averages \(p/n\); a common flag is \(h_{ii} > 3p/n\).

$$D_i = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^n (\hat{y}_j - \hat{y}_{j(i)})^2}{p \, \hat{\sigma}^2}$$

What this says in words: refit the model with observation \(i\) deleted, and ask how far all the fitted values moved. That is Cook's distance — influence measured directly. Conventional threshold: \(D_i > 4/n\).

mean leverage        0.00019  (= p/n = 0.00019)
obs above 3x mean    457
max Cook's D         5.2624
obs above 4/n        941

A Cook's distance of 5.26 is enormous — the threshold here is \(4/20{,}640 \approx 0.0002\).

Nine of the ten have AveRooms above the 99th percentile (10.36). The median block group has 5.23 rooms per household. Six of the ten have populations under 100.

Those numbers are not houses. AveRooms is total rooms ÷ households. A block group with a handful of households and any institutional building — a dormitory, a barracks, a hotel — produces an average-rooms figure that describes nothing real.

The most influential observations are a measurement artifact, and the artifact is a small denominator.

That Reframes the Whole Finding

The negative AveRooms coefficient was substantially a fact about tiny block groups containing large buildings, not about large houses.

This is Chapter 1's insistence that data is a product of measurement decisions, arriving thirteen chapters later as a diagnostic — discovered by the regression itself, without being told to look.

And it is a better result than the coefficient was.

Misconception: “influential observations are outliers, and outliers should be removed.”

Deleting points because they are inconvenient is how you produce a result that replicates on nobody else's data. An influential point is usually either a data error worth fixing at source, or the most informative observation you have.

F. What Diagnostics Cannot Save You From

Seven minutes • §14.5 • the limits

Back to the Map — the Bottom Two Rows

Heteroscedasticityunbiasedfixed — HC3
Clustered dependenceunbiasedfixed — cluster-robust
High leverage / influencefragilehandled — report the concentration

Omitted variables

\(\hat{\beta}\) converges to the wrong number — Ch 13's OVB formula says which wrong number. HC3 around a biased estimate gives you a precise interval around the wrong value, which is worse than an imprecise one because it is more persuasive.

Wrong functional form

Fit a line to a curve and the coefficient is a weighted average of slopes you did not intend. This one at least announces itself — if you look. Next slide.

A Very Precise Interval Around a Meaningless Number

\(R^2 = 0.90\), HC3 SE = 0.0058. Most analysts would ship this. The slope of 0.648 corresponds to nothing — the true relationship has no constant slope. Refit in \(\log x\) and the slope recovers 3.005, essentially the truth. No cov_type argument would have found this. Only looking at the residuals does.

Key Concept

Two kinds of failure, one chapter.

Heteroscedasticity, clustered dependence and leverage break your inference while leaving the estimate intact — fixable with HC3, cluster-robust errors, and clear reporting.

Omitted variables and misspecified functional form break the estimate, and every diagnostic in this chapter will pass while you are wrong.

Knowing which kind of failure you face is more valuable than any single test.

Economist at Work: A Literature Audits Itself

For two decades, economic history produced persistence results — modern outcomes across regions traced to characteristics of those same places centuries earlier. The \(t\)-statistics were often enormous.

Conley and Kelly noticed that variables measured across space — soil quality, ruggedness, distance to a coast, modern income — all trend strongly: nearby places resemble each other far more than chance would produce. Regress one such variable on another and you can get a huge \(t\) from two series with nothing to do with each other.

The important part is what they did next. Rather than argue from theory, they built two diagnostics a persistence study can be made to fail: a placebo test replacing the historical treatment with pure spatial noise (if the method is sound, noise should find nothing), and a synthetic outcomes test. They applied both to 30 studies in leading journals. Few approached significance.

The corrections from §B and §C were often already applied in those papers — and were not enough.

The problem was in the design, not the variance. The remedy is a design change, not an estimator change.

Conley, T. G. & Kelly, M. (2025). “The Standard Errors of Persistence.” Journal of International Economics 153.

G. A Diagnostic Workflow

Five minutes • §14.6 • the part you photograph

The Slide to Photograph

1

Plot residuals against fitted values. Always, before anything else. A funnel is variance; a pattern in the middle is functional form. Cost: three lines. Catches more real problems than every formal test today combined.

2

Use HC3 by default. Not “if Breusch–Pagan rejects.” By default. Run the formal tests to report the diagnosis, not to decide whether to be careful.

3

Ask how the treatment was assigned and cluster there. Answerable before you touch the data, and it has by far the largest consequences of anything today. Recall the 85%.

4

Check leverage and influence. Cook's distance, look at the top ten, refit without them. If the conclusion changes, that is a finding to report — and look at what those observations are.

5

Ask what would break the estimate rather than the interval. An unmeasurable confounder? A non-linear relationship? Neither shows up above, and both are worse than everything above.

The Actual Deliverable

Diagnostics are not a private ritual performed before publication. They are part of the result.

“Standard errors are HC3-robust; clustered at the state level, which is the level of policy assignment; the estimate is stable to dropping the ten most influential observations.”

Three clauses that tell a reader precisely how much to believe you. Their absence tells them something too.

Being able to write those three clauses is the actual deliverable of this hour.

Key Takeaways

1. Every standard error is the output of a formula assuming equal-sized, unrelated errors — and residuals are observable, so those assumptions are testable.

2. Heteroscedasticity, clustering and leverage corrupt inference, not the estimate. HC3 left every California coefficient unchanged and collapsed one \(t\) from −11.23 to −2.25.

3. Cluster at the level of treatment assignment. A region-level policy with no true effect was called significant 84.7% of the time by naive standard errors and 6.3% by clustered ones.

4. Report how concentrated a result is: ten rows of 20,640 held 85.2% of the influence, and dropping them doubled the coefficient.

5. No diagnostic here detects an omitted confounder or a wrong functional form. Those corrupt the estimate, every test today will pass, and the fix is a better design.
