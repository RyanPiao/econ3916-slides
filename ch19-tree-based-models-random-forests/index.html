Three Fixes From Monday

Three things from Monday, said more carefully.

1. AUC is not accuracy. AUC 0.811 is the share of recession–calm pairs the model ranks the right way round: 14,222 of 17,532. It measures ranking, nothing more.

2. Lab 18's rows are recall targets. 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 are how much of the fraud each model must catch, not how common fraud is. At recall 0.6 the gap is −0.018 with an interval of −0.047 to +0.025: it includes zero, so do not act on it (Part 2, question 2).

3. Ridge and lasso do not fix a changed world. They, and today's forests, trade a little bias for less variance. That helps with noise. The 2022–24 inversion was the world changing over time, and none of them fixes that.

Decision Trees

The Nonparametric Extension of OLS

Decision Trees = "20 Questions" for Data

How does a tree turn a row into a prediction?

At each node the tree asks one yes-or-no question: “Is income > $50,000?” “Is it downtown?” “Are reviews > 4.5?”

Node: one question.
Leaf: where the questions stop; it predicts the average of the training rows that land in it.
Depth: how many questions a row answers before it reaches a leaf.

OLS: one global line through all the data.
Tree: rectangles in feature space, each with its own prediction. No linearity assumed.

Gini Impurity: The Split Criterion

How do we score how mixed a group is?

$$G = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k^2$$

\(p_k\): the share of the node's rows in class \(k\).

  • \(G = 0\): pure, every row in one class
  • \(G = 0.5\): the most mixed two classes can be (50/50)

Work it first (30 seconds): a node with 80 positives and 20 negatives.

\(G = 1 - (0.80^2 + 0.20^2) = 1 - 0.68 = 0.32\)

Why Squaring Punishes a Mixed Node

Why square the shares?

Squaring rewards concentration. A node that is nearly all one class has a large ∑p², so G = 1 − ∑p² falls toward zero — which is what pushes the tree toward clean separation.

How the Tree Chooses Splits

Which split does the tree pick? Work one.

$$\Delta G = G(\text{parent}) - \left[\frac{n_L}{n}\, G(\text{left}) + \frac{n_R}{n}\, G(\text{right})\right]$$

Weighted: 0.6 × 0.0644 + 0.4 × 0.495 = 0.237

Reduction: 0.32 − 0.237 = 0.083. The tree tries every feature and every threshold and keeps the biggest reduction.

Greedy: the best split right now. No looking ahead.

Predicting a number (Lab 19's house values): score a split by how much it lowers the squared error inside the two groups. Same idea, spread in place of mixing.

Memorising the Noise

Why is a perfect score on the training rows a warning?

Let a tree grow until every leaf is pure (15,870 leaves for 16,512 rows in Lab 19, almost one block group per leaf) and training error goes to zero. It has not learned the housing market — it has memorised this sample, down to one 42-year-old block at one latitude.

That gap is variance. A score on the training set is not evidence of anything.

Single Trees: Flexible but Unstable

If one tree is unstable, what does averaging many of them buy?

One California block group. Grow a model on each of 20 different samples of 8,000 other block groups and ask each for this block group's value (in $100,000s).

Why it works: if B independent estimates each have variance σ², their average has variance σ²/B. The idea behind Topic 7's standard error: averages vary less.

Averaging removes the scatter, not the miss: the forests still sit below 2.48. That part is bias.

How Bagging Works

How do we get many different trees from one data set?

Bagging = bootstrap aggregating, in three steps:

\(\hat{f}_{\text{bag}}(x) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat{f}_b(x)\)

The 37% You Left Out Is a Free Test Set

Can the left-out rows grade the forest for free?

Each tree is grown on its own bootstrap sample, so each tree has its own leftovers — rows it never saw. Score the tree on those and you get a validation score without holding anything back up front.

The Correlation Floor

Why does averaging 1,000 trees not drive the variance to zero?

$$\text{Var}(\bar{f}) = \underbrace{\rho\sigma^2}_{\text{FLOOR}} + \underbrace{\frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2}_{\text{shrinks with B}}$$

\(\rho\): how alike two trees’ predictions are  ·  \(\sigma^2\): one tree’s variance  ·  \(B\): the number of trees

First term \(\rho\sigma^2\): Stays no matter how many trees. This is the floor.

Second term: Shrinks toward 0 as B → ∞. This is the averaging benefit.

With σ² fixed, the only way lower is to reduce ρ. How?

Poll: How Low Can Averaging Go?

Trees with variance σ² = 0.50 and correlation ρ = 0.85. Average B = 100 of them. What is the lowest variance more trees could ever reach?

Previous page: Var = ρσ² + (1 − ρ)σ² / B.

A) 0.005
B) 0.050
C) 0.425
D) 0.500

Answer: C — 0.425

The correlation floor: ρ × σ² = 0.85 × 0.50 = 0.425 . Even with infinite trees, the floor remains. With σ² fixed, the only way to lower it is to reduce ρ — which is what a random forest does.

Key Insight: Averaging removes the second term, never the first. To go lower, lower ρ.

Numerical Example: Bagging

With trees this alike, what does averaging 100 of them reach?

\(\sigma^2 = 0.50\), \(B = 100\) trees, \(\rho = 0.85\) (bagging)

\(\text{Var} = 0.85 \times 0.50 + \frac{0.15}{100} \times 0.50 = 0.425 + 0.001 = \mathbf{0.426}\)

The floor is 0.425. Adding more trees barely helps — we are stuck.

To go lower, we need to attack ρ.

Random Forests

Breaking the Correlation Floor (Breiman, 2001)

Hiding Features on Purpose

How do we make the trees disagree?

At every split, the tree may only look at a random subset of the p features (Lab 19 has p = 8). Starve it of the dominant predictor often enough and the trees stop agreeing — which is the ρ you are trying to lower.

Check this in the lab. √p is RandomForestClassifier's default. RandomForestRegressor does not default to p/3 — scikit-learn uses max_features=1.0, every feature, so the trees stay correlated unless you set it yourself.

When the Simpler Model Is the Right Call

When would you still choose the simpler model?

Accuracy. Lab 19, same held-out rows: ridge R² 0.58, random forest 0.80. The forest predicts better.

Reading the model. Ridge gives you 8 coefficients you can read out to a regulator. The forest averages 100 unrestricted trees, and one unrestricted tree on these rows already has 15,870 leaves.

The call. Where each decision must be explained (credit, lending), the readable model can be worth the lost accuracy.

Common Misconception

Can a forest overfit by having too many trees?

"More trees = overfitting"

Reality: Adding trees does NOT increase overfitting. The \((1-\rho)/B\) term decreases monotonically.

RF CAN overfit through:

  • max_depth too high (trees memorize)
  • max_features too close to p (trees correlate)

n_estimators is the one hyperparameter you can safely increase.

Two Ways to Ask “Which Feature Mattered?”

Which features did the forest lean on? Two ways to ask.

1. Mean decrease in impurity (MDI)

Add up each feature's drops in impurity at every split it was used (Gini for classes, squared error for numbers). Free: it comes out of training.

Blind spot: a feature with many possible cut points, like average occupancy or population, gets more chances to look useful.

2. Permutation importance

Shuffle one column of the test rows, re-score the fitted model, and see how far the score falls. It measures what the model actually loses.

Blind spot: shuffle one of two related columns, like latitude and longitude, and the model leans on the other, so both look smaller.

Compare them by rank: the scales differ. Lab 19 Part 2 asks where its two lists disagree, and why.

SHAP splits one prediction into pushes: start at the average prediction (0.27 here), each feature pushes it up (red) or down (blue), and the pushes add up to this borrower’s prediction. Simulated loans; pick a borrower from the dropdown. In Python: shap.TreeExplainer(model).shap_values(X)

Decision Scenario: The Feature Importance Trap

The forest ranks credit inquiries first. Should the bank cut them?

The bank’s own mortgage borrowers: a random forest predicts which of them will default in the next year, with an excellent AUC. Feature importance ranks recent credit inquiries (how often a borrower applied for other credit this year) #1.

The bank’s chief risk officer says: "Inquiries drive defaults. Stop our borrowers from applying for credit more than 3 times a year, and their defaults will fall."

What is wrong with this reasoning?

Poll: What Does Feature Importance Tell You?

A random forest ranks a feature first in importance. That tells you the feature:

A) causes the outcome
B) helps the model predict the outcome
C) is the one to intervene on
D) measures how confident the model is

Answer: B — What Predicts the Outcome

Importance measures what helps the model predict. A and C read prediction as cause; D mixes up importance with how confident the model is.

Neither importance is causal. Both answer “what is this model leaning on?”, never “what would change the outcome if we intervened?”

The bank: many credit inquiries signal money trouble that is already there. Stopping the applications hides the signal; it does not make anyone less likely to default. Using inquiries to flag borrowers early, and offer help, is fine: that is prediction.
