1  •  Four Numbers

Every metric you will ever meet is a ratio of the same four counts.

The Table Everything Comes From

Predicted +Predicted −
Actually +True positive (TP)False negative (FN)
Actually −False positive (FP)True negative (TN)

Four counts. Everything else is a ratio of some of them — and the metric's meaning is fixed by its denominator.

$$\text{Recall} = \text{TPR} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} \quad\; \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP} \quad\; \text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP+TN}$$

Same numerator in the first two. They differ only in what they divide by — and that difference is the entire content.

The Denominator Is the Question

Recall divides by everything that actually happened. “Of the 36 recessions, how many did we catch?”

The denominator is fixed by the world.

Precision divides by everything we predicted. “Of the times we sounded the alarm, how often were we right?”

The denominator is chosen by us — and it moves when we move the threshold.

FPR divides by everything that did not happen. “Of the 487 calm months, how many did we wrongly flag?”

Recall and precision answer questions two different people ask. A central banker who missed a downturn is asked about recall. A forecaster whose alarms keep not materialising is asked about precision. No threshold maximises both.

Numbers in the Cells

The same fitted model at the threshold Section 5 derives for a central banker, \(t = 0.099\).

Recall = 28/36 = 0.78
Precision = 28/125 = 0.22
FPR = 97/487 = 0.20
Accuracy = 418/523 = 0.80
\(F_1\) = 0.35

Every metric today reads off that one table. Nothing else is going on.

Both Summary Metrics Just Got Worse

Model that never fires

93.1%

accuracy — and catches 0 of 36 recessions

Model at \(t=0.099\)

79.9%

accuracy — and catches 28 of 36

The model is now really useful, and accuracy and \(F_1\) have both got worse. Two summary metrics, both pointing away from the model a decision-maker actually wants.

This is no curiosity: it is what happens whenever you optimise a symmetric metric on an asymmetric problem.

What \(F_1\) Is Hiding

$$F_1 = 2\cdot\frac{\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}$$

Interpretation. The harmonic mean punishes imbalance. Precision 1.0 with recall 0.01 has an arithmetic mean of 0.50 and an \(F_1\) of about 0.02. That is the right behaviour.

But note what it assumes: that precision and recall matter equally. That is a cost assumption, smuggled in as a formula.

So the advice is not to switch letters. Instead, if you can say how much more one error costs than the other, skip the summary metric and price the errors directly. That is Section 5.

Poll: Which Number Is That?

A model flags 125 months as “recession coming.” 28 were right, and there were 36 recessions in total. Your colleague reports: “78% — the model catches most recessions.”

Which metric is that, and what has the report left out?

A) Precision — it leaves out how many recessions were missed
B) Recall — it leaves out that 97 of the 125 alarms were false
C) Accuracy — it leaves out the base rate
D) FPR — it leaves out the size of the positive class

Answer: B — That Is Recall

\(28/36 = 0.78\) divides by everything that happened, so it is recall. The number is true and the sentence is misleading — a decision-maker acts on alarms, not on recessions, and 97 of 125 alarms were false.

Find the denominator first. It tells you which question the metric answers, and therefore whose problem it addresses.

2  •  The ROC Curve

A metric that does not depend on the threshold — and what it gives up to get there.

Sweep the Cutoff, Plot the Trade-off

from sklearn.metrics import roc_curve, roc_auc_score

fpr, tpr, thr = roc_curve(y, p)
roc_auc_score(y, p)   # 0.811

Chapter 17 computed one confusion matrix per cutoff. Sweep the cutoff continuously, plot TPR against FPR, and you have the whole set of trade-offs the model makes available.

Each marked point is one row of Chapter 17's threshold table — nothing new, just plotted.

What AUC Actually Measures

AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen positive case receives a higher predicted score than a randomly chosen negative case.

Interpretation. 0.811 means: pick a month that preceded a recession and a month that did not, and the model gives the higher probability to the right one about 81% of the time.

It is a measure of ranking quality, and nothing more.

Common misconception: “An AUC of 0.81 means the model is 81% accurate.” It says nothing about accuracy at any threshold, nothing about whether the probabilities are believable, and nothing about the cases you actually act on.

Two Properties, Both Consequential

AUC is threshold-independent.

It summarises the whole curve, so it is unaffected by the 0.5 default that ruined Chapter 17's accuracy figure. A real strength — it is why AUC held up in that chapter while accuracy did not.

AUC is invariant to any order-preserving transformation.

Halve every probability, square them, run them through any monotone function — the ranking is unchanged, so AUC is unchanged.

That is why AUC cannot see this morning's hook. It is mathematically incapable of detecting it, because the failure is in the level and AUC reads only the order.

Poll: Halve Every Probability

You take every predicted probability from our model and halve it: 0.42 becomes 0.21, 0.02 becomes 0.01. Nothing else changes.

What happens to the AUC?

A) It halves, to about 0.41
B) It falls, but by less than half
C) It is exactly unchanged at 0.811
D) It cannot be computed — the probabilities no longer sum correctly

Answer: C — Exactly Unchanged

Halving is order-preserving, so every pairwise comparison comes out the same way and the AUC is identical. Every probability is now wrong by a factor of two, and AUC reports no change at all.

You have just proved the next section in one keystroke. Ranking and level are separate things, and one common metric can only see one of them.

3  •  Precision-Recall

The right curve when the thing you care about is rare.

Same Model. Two Numbers.

AUC 0.811. Baseline 0.5. Roughly 1.6× a coin flip.

PR-AUC 0.188. Baseline is the base rate, 0.069. Roughly 2.7× random.

Same data, same predictions — and two numbers a reader would interpret completely differently. Both are correct.

Why They Diverge: The Denominator, Again

Suppose the model raises 50 alarms and 12 are right.

The same 38 mistakes, described as a small rate or a large fraction depending on what you divide by. Under class imbalance the negatives are numerous, so FPR is generously scaled and ROC looks good. Precision has no such cushion.

What It Costs to Be Useful

thresholdrecallprecision
0.1600.250.20
0.1300.500.24
0.1000.750.22

To catch half the recessions you must accept that roughly three-quarters of your alarms are false. To catch three-quarters of them, precision barely moves — it is already near the floor.

That sentence is what a decision-maker needs. No summary number provides it — not AUC, not \(F_1\), not PR-AUC. Only the curve, read at the operating point you intend to use.

Precision Is Not Portable

Recall and FPR are conditioned on the truth, so they are properties of the model and do not change when the base rate changes. Precision is conditioned on the predictions, so it does.

A fraud model validated where fraud runs at 0.5% and deployed where it runs at 0.1% shows the same recall and FPR and much worse precision. The analytics team reports a model that has “degraded” when nothing about it changed. The base rate moved.

So “our model achieves 40% precision” without the base rate it was measured at is close to meaningless — the same sentence describes an excellent model in one market and a poor one in another.

Report the base rate alongside precision. Always — for exactly the reason you report it alongside accuracy.

4  •  Ranking Is Not Level

Back to the twenty-one months.

Two Separate Achievements

Discrimination

The ability to rank. Do positives get higher scores than negatives?

AUC measures it. Our model is good at it.

Calibration

The accuracy of the level. Among cases assigned probability \(p\), does the event occur a fraction \(p\) of the time?

Our model fails it — in the worst possible place.

They are independent — and you proved it two minutes ago. Multiply every probability by 0.5: the ranking is untouched so AUC is identical, and every level is now half what it should be.

Which you need depends on the use. Only ranking things — triage a queue, prioritise audits — discrimination suffices. Putting the probability into a calculation — expected loss, a policy trigger — needs calibration, and AUC will not tell you whether you have it.

Measuring It: The Reliability Diagram

bins = [0, .05, .10, .15, .25, 1.0]
idx  = np.digitize(p, bins) - 1
# per bin: p[mask].mean()
#          y[mask].mean()

Interpretation. Perfect calibration puts every bin on the 45° line: what was promised equals what happened.

Five lines of code. It catches failures no rank-based metric can.

Bottom two bands: close, slightly conservative. Middle two: under-predict — the model says 18%, recessions followed 38% of the time. Top band: collapses entirely.

The Twenty-One Months, Located

Every one is the record 2022–24 inversion. The confident predictions are not spread thinly across forty years of occasional overreach — they are one episode, and Chapter 17 explained why: quantitative easing compressed the term premium, so an inversion of a given depth no longer meant what it had meant.

Chapter 17 found this from the feature side; we found it from the evaluation side. Accuracy missed it, AUC could not see it — only comparing predicted levels against realised frequencies surfaced it.

The Caveat, Before Any Conclusion

Twenty-one months is a small bin, and those months are not independent — they are consecutive months of one macroeconomic episode. A calibration table computed on autocorrelated data overstates its own sample size.

Fair reading

“This model's confident predictions are dominated by a single regime in which it was wrong.”

Overclaim

“The failure rate at high probabilities is precisely 0%.”

Everything today is in-sample. Chapter 20 supplies the machinery for evaluating models on dependent data. A lecture about careful evaluation that oversold its own evidence would contradict itself.

Poll: Which Check Catches It?

The model said one-in-three, twenty-one times, and nothing happened. Which check would have caught this failure?

A) A higher accuracy threshold
B) Comparing predicted probabilities against realised frequencies by band
C) Reporting AUC with a confidence interval
D) Refitting the model on more recent data

Answer: B — The Reliability Diagram

A is the metric we already ruled out. C gives you a better-measured ranking statistic — and ranking was never the problem. D might help eventually, but you would not know to do it until something told you the model was wrong.

More precision about the wrong quantity is still the wrong quantity.

What You Would Do About It

Recalibrate.

Fit a second, order-preserving model mapping raw scores to observed frequencies on held-out data. Because it is order-preserving it leaves AUC exactly unchanged while fixing the levels — a neat demonstration that the two properties really are separable.

But it will not save this model.

A correction fitted on 1982–2021 has no way to know that 2023's inversions were different in kind. Recalibration fixes systematic overconfidence; it does not fix a feature whose relationship to the outcome changed.

So the realistic response is narrower: caveat the regime explicitly, or widen the intervals, or — preserving a thirty-year track record — keep publishing the model unchanged and separately publish the explanation of why the current reading should be discounted. Sometimes the repair is changing what you say about the model.

5  •  The Threshold

Where the economics enters — and the missing ingredient is not statistical.

Stop Choosing a Metric. Price the Mistakes.

$$\text{Cost}(t) = C_{FP}\cdot FP(t) \;+\; C_{FN}\cdot FN(t)$$

Interpretation. As you lower \(t\) the model fires more often, so \(FP\) rises and \(FN\) falls. The optimum sits where one more false alarm costs exactly what one more avoided miss saves.

$$t^{*} = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}}$$

Interpretation. The cutoff is the false-alarm cost as a share of total error cost. Equal costs give \(t^{*} = 0.5\) — which is where the default came from. It is an assumption, not a neutral starting point.

Run It Backwards

$$\frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FN}} = \frac{t}{1-t}$$

Interpretation. Whatever cutoff you ship asserts an exchange rate between the two errors. A default of 0.5 asserts a false alarm and a miss cost the same. A cutoff of 0.1 asserts a miss is nine times worse.

The most portable habit in this course. Compute that ratio for the cutoff you are about to ship, say it out loud in one sentence, and ask whether you would defend it to the person who bears the cost.

If you would not, you have found your real threshold — by working backwards from a claim you are willing to defend.

From Lab 18: The Cost Curve

cost-minimising t   0.068
cost                0.4587
cost at default 0.5 0.9437

A simulated imbalanced problem where a miss costs ten times a false alarm.

Shipping the default would have cost 2.06× the optimum.

Two lessons. The optimum is nowhere near 0.5 — a 10:1 ratio pulls it towards \(1/11 \approx 0.09\). And the curve is flat near its minimum: anywhere in roughly 0.05–0.12 is nearly as good.

Three Decision-Makers, One Model

The central banker (1:9) confirms the theory almost exactly — 0.099 against a predicted 0.100 — catching 28 of 36 recessions for 97 false alarms over forty years. The model's highest predicted probability is 0.4231, so 0.5 and 0.8 are unreachable: the other two optima saturate at the corner and the classifier never fires.

The Corners Are the More Interesting Result

The formula has not failed. It is reporting something true: for a decision-maker with symmetric costs, or one who fears false alarms more than misses, this model should never trigger action.

A model can be informative and still be the wrong basis for a particular decision — and discovering that before deployment is worth more than any metric.

When the Threshold Is Not Yours to Choose

Network rules.

Card networks cap a merchant's chargeback ratio, with penalties escalating at each tier — and the programmes are reorganised periodically. A fraud team therefore maximises expected profit subject to that cap. The binding constraint sets the threshold instead of the cost ratio.

Model-risk governance.

Under supervisory guidance, a threshold is part of the model: changing it requires documentation, independent validation and monitoring. It is a governed parameter, not a free setting — an analyst who nudges it to improve a dashboard has made a regulatory filing inaccurate.

The exact numbers move. That the constraint comes from outside your loss function does not.

Poll: The Default Cutoff

Your team ships a fraud classifier at the default cutoff of 0.5, because “nobody had a better number.”

What have you asserted?

A) Nothing — 0.5 is the neutral choice
B) That a false decline and an undetected fraud cost the same
C) That the base rate of fraud is 50%
D) That the model is well calibrated

Answer: B — A One-to-One Exchange Rate

From \(C_{FP}/C_{FN} = t/(1-t)\), a cutoff of 0.5 asserts 1 : 1. There is no neutral cutoff — there is only a stated assumption and an unstated one.

And in fraud, as the next two slides show, 1:1 is not merely arbitrary. It is badly wrong — and wrong in the direction most teams guess incorrectly.

The Asymmetry Runs the Wrong Way

$231B

projected global e-commerce revenue lost to false declines, 2026

$56B

actual global card fraud losses, 2025

A projection and an actual, from different years and different methodologies — so quote the ratio loosely. But even read conservatively, merchants lose roughly four times more revenue to blocking legitimate customers than to the fraud the blocking exists to prevent.

Industry research (Datos Insights; LexisNexis True Cost of Fraud), not peer-reviewed work. Attribute it. The direction is the robust part — and the direction is what should change your threshold.

Work the Arithmetic for One Merchant

1,000,000 transactions • 0.3% fraudulent • average order $120 at a 25% margin ($30) • a fraudulent order costs about $553 all-in, from the $4.61-per-$1-of-fraud estimate.

Tighten to catch 90% of fraud at a 2% false-decline rate: roughly $2.6M of cost against $1.49M saved. The tightening destroys about a million dollars of value — and every number on the fraud team's dashboard improves while it happens.

The benefit is concentrated, measured and attributed to the fraud team. The cost is diffuse, delayed, and lands in another department's revenue line. The measurement asymmetry, not the cost asymmetry, is what makes this mistake persistent — Chapter 2's Goodhart problem in a machine-learning setting.

6  •  Fairness Is a Constraint

And you cannot satisfy all of it at once.

Computing an Impact Ratio

New York City's Local Law 144 requires an annual, published bias audit of automated employment decision tools — and it specifies the arithmetic.

$$\text{impact ratio} = \frac{0.180}{0.240} = 0.75$$

Interpretation. 0.75 is below 0.80, the EEOC's four-fifths rule, so this flags as potential disparate impact — and must be published. Nothing here says the model is wrong: if group B really has a lower rate of the outcome, a well-calibrated model should advance fewer of them.

You Cannot Satisfy All of It at Once

Except when base rates are equal across groups, or prediction is perfect, no model can simultaneously achieve:

Calibration within groups — a predicted 20% means 20% for everybody

Equal false positive rates across groups

Equal false negative rates across groups

Each is eminently reasonable, and someone will insist on each. Equalising false positive rates necessarily breaks calibration for at least one group; restoring calibration necessarily reintroduces an error-rate gap.

There is no technical escape, and pretending otherwise is the real failure. The choice belongs to the people accountable for the decision — informed by an analyst who made the trade-off explicit rather than resolving it with a default and saying nothing.

7  •  The Workflow

Seven steps, and the three you cannot skip.

An Evaluation Workflow

1

Report the base rate first. Accuracy has a floor at it, PR-AUC has its baseline at it, precision moves with it.

2

Never report accuracy alone. If you report it, put the base rate beside it.

3

Choose the curve by the class balance. ROC when balanced, precision-recall when positives are rare.

4

Check calibration if the probability will be used as a number. If it only orders things, you may skip it — but say that you did.

5

Derive the threshold from costs, and state the implied exchange rate. Then check it is feasible.

6

Disaggregate. Compute your headline metrics by subgroup.

7

Report the operating point, not just the area — and re-evaluate on a schedule.

If time is short, steps 1, 4 and 5 are the irreducible core. And fix the metric before you fit — choosing it after seeing the results means choosing the cost assumption that flatters you, which is model selection on the test set in another form.

Fraud is 0.2% of transactions. Your model reports 99.8% accuracy.

Topic 26 — Capstone.

Every project states its metric and its threshold justification. Five questions a reader will ask: what is the base rate; why this metric; what threshold and what exchange rate does it imply; is the model calibrated if you quote probabilities; does performance hold across the subgroups the decision affects.

A project that answers those five is defensible even if its model is modest. One that reports a single impressive number is not, however good the model turns out to be.

A model does not have a quality.
It has a table.

Every number you quote is a ratio of its cells, under an assumption about what your errors cost.
Make the assumption visible, and you have done the job.
