An Index Is a Compression Algorithm

A grocery store

30,000 → 1

The CPI compresses every shelf price into one number.

A stock market

30 → 1

The Dow compresses thirty share prices into one headline.

A product team

millions → 1

Every compression discards information. The index is not wrong for discarding — that is its job. It is wrong when what it discarded is the thing you needed.

The Laspeyres Price Index

A Laspeyres index prices one fixed basket — what people bought in the base period — at today's prices, and compares that to what the same basket cost back then.

IN WORDS

$$\text{Index}_t = \frac{\text{cost of the base-period basket at } \textbf{today's} \text{ prices}}{\text{cost of the same basket at } \textbf{base-period} \text{ prices}} \times 100$$

IN SYMBOLS

$$CPI_t = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} P_{t,i} \times Q_{0,i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k} P_{0,i} \times Q_{0,i}} \times 100$$

\( P_{t,i} \) — price of good \( i \) today

\( P_{0,i} \) — price of good \( i \) in the base period

\( Q_{0,i} \) — base-period quantity. Frozen. This is the whole story.

\( k \) — number of goods in the basket

The base period index equals 100 by construction — it is a definition, not a finding.

What That Formula Actually Says

“How much would it cost today to buy exactly what a typical consumer bought in the base year?”

The strength

Freezing quantities makes the index simple to compute and comparable across time. Only prices move, so any change you see is a price change.

The weakness

Freezing quantities ignores substitution. Real people switch away from things that get expensive. The index assumes they do not.

Same design choice. Both consequences. That is what a measurement decision looks like.

Poll: Which Line Moves the Index?

In our three-good basket, gasoline rose 44%, bread rose 40%, and rent rose 30%.

Which line contributes the most to the 31.6% CPI increase?

A) Gasoline — it had the largest percentage increase
B) Rent — it has by far the largest budget share
C) Bread — it is the cheapest, so it changes fastest

Answer: B — Weight Beats Percentage

Gasoline had the biggest percentage move and still supplies only an eighth of the increase.

Common mistake (A): a percentage change feels like the important quantity because it is the number printed next to the price. But in a Laspeyres index a price change matters in proportion to spending share. This is exactly why shelter dominates the real CPI too — and why we look at the component weights next.

The Same Basket, in Code

import pandas as pd

basket = pd.DataFrame(
    {"q0": [200, 600, 12], "p0": [3.00, 2.50, 1200], "p1": [4.20, 3.60, 1560]},
    index=["bread", "gasoline", "rent"])

cost_2020 = basket.p0 * basket.q0      # 600, 1500, 14400  → 16,500
cost_2024 = basket.p1 * basket.q0      # 840, 2160, 18720  → 21,720

cost_2020 / cost_2020.sum()            # share of the spending: rent 0.87

Three lines of output, and they are the three numbers on the slide before this one: 131.6, rent 87% of the spending, rent 83% of the rise. Same arithmetic you just did on paper.

The Cost of Freezing the Basket

What the index assumes

Same beef, at the new price, all year → measured cost up a lot

What people do

Switch to chicken → true cost up less

The gap = substitution bias

Substitution bias. A Laspeyres index systematically overstates the true cost-of-living increase, because it holds quantities fixed while consumers move. It is the most studied limitation of the CPI — and it is not a bug in the data, it is a consequence of the formula.

Hold that thought. We will put a number on it in Part 2 — the Boskin Commission's.

What the BLS Actually Does

Same formula you just used. Vastly different data operation.

This is not a web scrape. When a sampled store closes, a person chooses the replacement outlet — same geography, similar demographics. When a laptop model is discontinued, that person decides whether the price gap is a price change or a quality change.

The formula is the easy part — one ratio, and you just did it on paper. Everything hard about the CPI is a judgement call someone made before the arithmetic starts.

The Hard Part: Quality Adjustment

A laptop costs the same as last year but has twice the processing power. Did the price stay flat — or effectively fall?

Where it works

The BLS uses hedonic regression — decomposing a price into the value of its measurable characteristics: screen size, clock speed, RAM, storage. Technology products have exactly those.

Where it struggles

Apparel, medical care and housing. What is the “characteristic vector” of a hospital stay? Quality adjustment there is contested, and the choices move the published number.

We are naming hedonics as a BLS practice, not estimating one. You already have the tools to read the idea: it is a regression of price on features — which is Chapter 12.

What Is Actually in the Basket

Shelter is more than a third of the index — rent plus owners' equivalent rent — bigger than food and medical care combined.

The live numbers: BLS Table 1, every category with its weight, updated monthly  ·  relative importance tables

Weights come from the Consumer Expenditure Survey — about 24,000 households, 200+ spending categories.

They are updated every year, from spending data about two years old. That lag is a measurement problem all by itself — we come back to it as Goodhart's third mechanism.

Poll 1's lesson, at national scale: the biggest weight wins, not the biggest percentage move.

Housing: The BLS Does Not Track House Prices

A house is an investment asset. The CPI measures consumption.

So the BLS skips house prices and prices the shelter instead — owners' equivalent rent.

The OER premise: if you were renting your own house from yourself, what would you pay in the current market?

The BLS asks homeowners exactly that — and samples rents across existing tenancies, not just new leases.

Sampling Every Lease Means Measuring Late

Most tenants are mid-lease and paying last year's rent, so a spike in new-lease rents reaches the index only as those leases renew: the CPI's rent numbers trail the market by 12–18 months.

The vibecession's second cause: separate from rate vs. level, and it hits the heaviest third of the basket.

A Different Index, A Different Rule

CPI

Cost-weighted. Goods count in proportion to household spending.

Dow Jones

Price-weighted. Stocks count in proportion to their share price.

S&P 500

Cap-weighted. Companies count in proportion to market value.

The Dow's rule has a strange consequence. A $200 stock carries four times the weight of a $50 stock — regardless of how big the two companies are. And when a stock splits, the sum of prices falls even though nothing economic has happened.

Same 30 companies. Change the weighting rule and you get a different index — and a different story about the market.

Poll: The Morning After a Split

A Dow component trading at $200 announces a 2-for-1 split. Each share becomes two $100 shares. Nothing else about the company changes.

What happens to the Dow Jones Industrial Average the next morning?

A) It falls about 33% — the price sum dropped
B) Unchanged — the divisor is reset to absorb the split
C) It rises — there are now twice as many shares
D) Unchanged — the Dow weights by market cap, which did not move

Answer: B — The Divisor Does the Work

The divisor is reset from 0.1520 to 0.1013 precisely so the index prints the same value across the split. Maintenance, not markets.

A is the common mistake — it is exactly what would happen without the reset, which is why the divisor exists. D is the subtler mistake: right answer, wrong reason. The Dow is price-weighted, not cap-weighted. The S&P 500 is the cap-weighted one. If you got the right answer from the wrong model, you will get the next question wrong.

The Divisor Exists to Absorb Arithmetic

Two stocks: A at $200, B at $100. Sum $300, divisor 0.152, index \( 300/0.152 = 1{,}974 \).

A splits 2-for-1. Market value is identical. But the price sum drops to $200, and the old divisor prints 1,316 — a 33% crash caused by nothing.

So the committee resets the divisor: \( d_{new} = d_{old} \times \frac{200}{300} = 0.1013 \), and the index prints 1,974 again.

The real divisor is about 0.16 today, so a $1 move in any Dow stock moves the index roughly 6 points. It was 16.67 when the Dow went to 30 stocks in 1928, and has been cut after every split and swap since.

Four Questions Before You Trust an Index

Before you trust any index — in this course or in a job — ask four questions.

1

What is in the basket?  CPI: 80,000 items. Dow: 30 stocks.

2

How are the components weighted?  Spending share, price, or market cap — three different answers.

3

When was the basket last updated?  CPI: every year, from two-year-old spending data. Dow: whenever a committee decides.

4

Who benefits from the number being high or low?  Social Security recipients want it high. Employers citing raises want it low.

These four recur in every chapter from here — through R² in Chapter 12 and feature importance in Chapter 19.

Real vs. Nominal

The single most common operation in applied economics

Who Is Richer?

Your grandfather, 1970

$5,000

a year — and he thought he was doing well

You, today

$55,000

a year — and you worry about rent

The naive comparison says you earn eleven times more. Is that right?

The correct comparison has to adjust for the fact that a 1970 dollar bought far more than a 2026 dollar. Do the adjustment before you draw the conclusion.

Put Both Numbers in the Same Dollars

CPI, 1970 average

38.8

CPI, 2020 average

258.9

CPI, 2025 average

322.0

$$\text{Real}_{2025} = \frac{5{,}000}{38.8} \times 322.0 = 41{,}445$$

His $5,000 had the purchasing power of about $41,400 in 2025 dollars. Against your $55,000, that is a real gain of roughly 33% — not 1,000%.

Money illusion is the gap between 1,000% and 33%.

The Error Everyone Makes Once

Both numbers must be in the same dollars before you divide. Deflate his 1970 income into 2020 dollars and then compare it to your 2025 nominal salary, and you are dividing two different currencies.

Mixed units

$55,000 (2025 nominal) ÷ $33,322 (2020 dollars) = +65%

Inflated by five years of price level that was never removed from the numerator.

Matched units

$44,220 (2020 dollars) ÷ $33,322 (2020 dollars) = +33%

Same answer as deflating both to 2025 dollars. The base year cancels.

Lab 2, Step 4 prints both numbers. 33% is the like-for-like comparison; 65% is the mistake on the left, printed on purpose. Your write-up says which dollars each number is in — and which one answers the question.

The Deflation Formula

$$\text{Real Value}_t = \frac{\text{Nominal Value}_t}{CPI_t} \times 100$$

Plain reading: a real value of $25.00 in 1982–84 dollars means the wage, after stripping out price-level change, buys what $25.00 bought in 1982–84.

Multiply by 100 and you land in base-period dollars (1982–84 for CPIAUCSL).

Multiply by the base-year CPI instead and you land in that year's dollars. This is what deflate_series() does.

The CPI is a weighted mean. Compare it to the simplest index of all, the sample mean \( \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \), which gives every observation equal weight. Choosing between equal and unequal weighting is itself a measurement decision.

Your First Reusable Function

def deflate_series(nominal, cpi, base_year=2020):
    base_cpi = cpi.loc[cpi.index.year == base_year].mean()
    aligned  = pd.DataFrame({"nominal": nominal, "cpi": cpi}).dropna()
    real     = (aligned["nominal"] / aligned["cpi"]) * base_cpi
    real.name = f"real_{base_year}"
    return real

Line 1. Take the base year's average CPI, not one month. A single month carries seasonal noise into every value.

Line 2. Align on shared dates and drop the mismatch. Wages start in 1964; CPI starts in 1947.

Line 3. Divide, then rescale. Whole columns divide element by element — no loop.

You will import this again in Chapters 4, 12, 16 and 20. Write it once, write it right.

Two Structural Breaks

A structural break is a point where the underlying process shifts abruptly. This chart has two — and both are invisible in the nominal series.

1973 — the peak, then two decades down

Oil shocks in 1973 and 1979, the end of Bretton Woods in 1971, and a productivity slowdown. Real wages then fell for two decades.

2020 — the jump

Largely a composition effect: low-wage workers lost their jobs first, which lifts the average without anyone getting a raise.

Money illusion hides structural change. The nominal line is smooth through both events. Deflating is what makes the breaks visible at all.

Poll: The Raise That Was Not

Over ten years, your nominal hourly wage doubles. Over the same ten years, the CPI also doubles.

What happened to your purchasing power?

A) It doubled — your paycheck is twice as large
B) Unchanged — the raise exactly offset the price level
C) It halved — prices ate the raise and then some
D) Cannot tell without knowing the base year

Answer: B — The Ratio Is Unchanged

Real wage is nominal divided by CPI. Both terms doubled, so the ratio is identical: \( \frac{2w}{2p} = \frac{w}{p} \). You buy exactly what you bought before.

A is money illusion in its purest form — and it is the mechanism behind the vibecession: workers who got 3–4% nominal raises after 2021 felt cheated because they were, in real terms. D is tempting and wrong: the base year rescales every value by the same constant. It changes the units on the axis; it can never change a ratio of two real values.

Misconception: “The CPI Measures the Cost of Living”

It measures a fixed basket, which is not the same thing as a true cost-of-living index. A true COLI would let consumers substitute.

Put a number on it

The Boskin Commission (1996) concluded the CPI overstated true cost-of-living increases by 0.8–1.6 percentage points per year.

The partial fix

The Chained CPI lets the basket update, correcting for substitution. It still cannot capture quality change, brand-new goods, or outlet substitution.

So when you hear “inflation was 3%,” the question is not just 3% of what? but 3% for whom, buying what, compared to what alternative?

Misconception: “Wages Have Been Stagnant Since the 1970s”

True — in real terms

Real AHE peaked in the early 1970s at $24.43 and had not durably regained that level by 2019 ($23.71, both in 2020 dollars).

Invisible — in nominal terms

Nominal AHE went from $2.50 to $32.53 over the same period. Quote that series and “stagnation” looks like nonsense.

Which claim you can defend depends entirely on which series and which deflator you chose. So state both. An unlabelled dollar figure is not an argument.

This is the professional habit today is really teaching: never present a monetary series without saying real or nominal, and in which year's dollars.

A US Big Mac cost $2.24 in April 2000. It costs $6.22 in July 2026. Is the burger really more expensive — or is that just the dollar shrinking?

You already have the tool. It is the same one you used on wages: divide by the CPI.

Three-Quarters of It Was the Dollar

Nominal: $2.24 → $6.22. That is +178%.

Real (2020 dollars): $3.39 → $4.84. That is +43%.

Roughly three-quarters of the sticker increase was inflation, not a more expensive hamburger.

Look at 2021–22: the sticker rose from $4.89 to $5.15, yet the real price fell, $4.82 → $4.52. A price that rises slower than the price level is a real price cut.

But the Real Price Did Rise

43% real growth is not nothing. Why would a burger get really more expensive?

Tradeable inputs

Beef, wheat, packaging. Priced in global markets. If they get expensive here, you can source them elsewhere.

Non-tradeable inputs

Restaurant rent, local labour, utilities. Priced locally. You cannot import a Boston storefront from a cheaper region.

Non-tradeables have inflated faster than the overall CPI, because they cannot be sourced away. That is Baumol's cost disease showing up in a hamburger: as productivity rises elsewhere in the economy, local services whose productivity barely moves — the person at the counter — get relatively more expensive over time.

Part Four · 7 minutes

When Indexes Fail

Goodhart's Law, and the counter-metric that was Goodharted too

Goodhart, 1975

Working at the Bank of England, Charles Goodhart noticed that whenever the central bank targeted a monetary aggregate — M3, say — that aggregate immediately stopped being a reliable indicator. Banks found ways to move money in and out of the measured category without changing any underlying activity.

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

The popular phrasing — anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, 1997, summarizing Goodhart

The cause is optimization, not dishonesty. When people know they are measured on a specific metric, they optimize that metric — not necessarily the behaviour it was designed to capture.

The Signature of a Goodharted Metric

Before the target. The metric and the thing it measures move together. That is why it was chosen — it was a good proxy.

After the target. The reported number keeps climbing. The underlying reality does not. Nobody had to lie for this to happen.

The diagnostic problem: from inside the organization you only see the blue line. The orange line is not measured — that is the whole reason you were using a proxy.

Schematic, not data. It names the shape so you can recognise it in a real dashboard.

Three Mechanisms, Three Different Fixes

MechanismWhat happensExampleFix
1. Optimization
against the measure		 The index has fiscal consequences, so its construction attracts pressure CPI drives Social Security COLAs, TIPS and tax brackets — 0.1 pp moves billions Transparency about methodology; publish alternative indexes
2. Gaming
the measure		 Agents inflate the number without improving the behaviour DAU/MAU lifted by notification spam; UK universities poaching star researchers before REF assessment Counter-metrics — a second measurement that detects the decoupling
3. Obsolescence
of the measure		 The world moves; the basket does not Gym memberships still weighted through the pandemic while home-office spend surged More frequent weight updates; population-specific indexes (CPI-E, CPI-W)

Diagnosing which mechanism is running is what determines the fix. A counter-metric does nothing about obsolescence. Faster reweighting does nothing about gaming.

Tech KPIs Are Indexes Too

DAU/MAU — daily active users over monthly active users, the “stickiness ratio.” A value of 0.50 means half of the month's users show up on a given day. Facebook has historically run near 0.65, among the highest in the industry.

Misconception: “a higher DAU/MAU always means better engagement.” It measures visit frequency, not visit quality. A notification-spam strategy inflates DAU while creating zero user value. Always pair a frequency metric with a quality metric.

Run the four index questions on it: what is in the basket, how is it weighted, when was it updated, who benefits?

Lifetime Value, and Its Two Lies

$$LTV = \frac{ARPU}{\text{monthly churn rate}}$$

Plain reading: with average revenue per user of $12.99 a month and 5% of subscribers cancelling each month, \( LTV = 12.99 / 0.05 = 259.80 \) — the revenue you expect from one subscriber before they leave.

Lie 1: churn is constant

It is not. Churn is front-loaded — new subscribers leave far faster than loyal ones.

Lie 2: ARPU is constant

It moves with pricing changes, upsells and discounts, none of which the formula sees.

A fine first approximation — and a Goodhart trap the moment it becomes a target. Teams “improve LTV” by redefining churn (counting a paused subscription as active) rather than by retaining anyone.

DAU/MAU: The Metric That Can Measure Notifications

Picture a product team paid on DAU/MAU. The cheapest way to lift it is not a better product — it is more notifications, tuned to force a daily open. Features could be prioritized by their ability to drive opens, whether or not those opens meant anything.

What went up

Notification frequency — and with it, the reported DAU/MAU.

What did not

Anything a user would recognise as value. Most people find the extra pings irritating.

Mechanism 2, exactly as the table describes it. So what is the fix? The theory says: build a counter-metric.

Meta Had Already Built the Fix — in 2018

January 2018 — three years before the Facebook Files. Meta re-ranked News Feed around “Meaningful Social Interactions” (MSI), explicitly to shift the objective from passive consumption toward real interaction between people. This is exactly the repair the theory prescribes.

And it became the second Goodhart failure. Because MSI scored reshares and comment threads heavily, the ranking system learned that the reliable way to manufacture “meaningful interaction” was content that provoked argument. The 2021 internal research found the metric was amplifying divisive material — and that Meta's own data scientists had raised it internally.

The hard lesson. A counter-metric is still a metric. It compresses, it becomes a target, and it can be optimized into a failure of its own. Repairing an index is maintenance you keep doing. The fix needs checking too.

A DAU/MAU team inflates a digital number. Wells Fargo fabricated actual bank accounts.

Poll: Which Counter-Metric Catches It?

Wells Fargo's cross-sell ratio kept rising for over a decade while the behaviour it was meant to capture was being destroyed.

Which counter-metric would have caught it earliest?

A) Total accounts opened per branch per quarter
B) Share of new accounts opened at the customer's own initiative
C) Average revenue per household
D) Employee satisfaction survey scores

Answer: B — It Moves the Opposite Way

The fraudulent accounts were bank-initiated by construction. So as the headline ratio climbed, the customer-initiated share would have collapsed — a visible divergence, exactly the shape on the Goodhart chart.

A fails because it is the primary metric with a different denominator — the same gaming moves it the same direction. C is contaminated: fee revenue rose on the fake accounts too. D is lagging and noisy, and would have surfaced years late. The rule: a counter-metric must be hard to move by the same behaviour that games the primary metric.

Key Takeaways

An index is a compression. What is in the basket, how it is weighted, and when it was updated shape every conclusion drawn from it.

Laspeyres freezes the basket. That gives comparability across time at the cost of a known upward substitution bias.

Never compare dollars across time without deflating — and make sure both sides are in the same dollars before you divide.

Tech KPIs are indexes with the same anatomy and the same failure modes as the CPI.

Goodhart's Law. When a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure — and the counter-metric you build to fix it becomes a target too.
