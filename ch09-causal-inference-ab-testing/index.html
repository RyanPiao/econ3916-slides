1  •  Why Randomisation Works

One equation, two terms. The whole chapter is about making the second one zero.

The Counterfactual You Cannot Have

A new checkout button. One user — call her Priya.

WORLD 1

Priya sees the new button. She buys.

This is \(Y_i(1)\).

WORLD 0

Priya sees the old button. She does not.

This is \(Y_i(0)\).

Priya lives in exactly one of them. The other outcome is the counterfactual, and it never happened, so no measurement can recover it. This is the Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference (Holland 1986).

The Notation We Will Use All Term

\(Y_i(1)\)

outcome if \(i\) is treated

\(Y_i(0)\)

outcome if \(i\) is not

\(D_i\)

1 if treated, 0 if control

$$\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$$

Interpretation: the difference for the same person, at the same moment, with and without the treatment. Never observable for anyone, ever.

The Retreat to the Average

$$\tau = E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)] = E[Y_i(1)] - E[Y_i(0)]$$

Interpretation: the Average Treatment Effect. Individual effects stay unknowable; their average does not.

What you gain

An expectation over a population is estimable from two group means.

What you give up

Any claim about a particular user. "It works" now means "on average, here".

What a Naive Comparison Actually Measures

Compare the treated to the untreated and you do not get the ATE. You get the ATE plus something else:

$$\underbrace{E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0]}_{\textstyle \text{what you compute}} = \underbrace{E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)]}_{\textstyle \text{ATE}} + \underbrace{E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i{=}1] - E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i{=}0]}_{\textstyle \text{selection bias}}$$

Term 1 — the ATE. The causal effect. What you are trying to learn.

Term 2 — selection bias. How different the two groups' outcomes would have been even with no treatment at all.

Chapter 6 wrote this as \(\text{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0\) — the same object, in the language of sampling. Term 2 can be large. On the data we are about to look at, it is nine times larger than the effect itself.

Every causal method in this course is a different strategy for making Term 2 zero.

The Same Programme, Two Answers

LaLonde's National Supported Work data, the Dehejia–Wahba sample you met in Chapter 8: n = 445 — 185 treated, 260 randomised controls.

Mean 1978 earnings: $6,349 treated vs $4,555 control → ATE +$1,794.

Swap the randomised controls for a general‑population comparison group and the same programme appears to cut earnings — by about $15,205 against PSID‑1, and by $14,784 in the lab's simulated version.

Same programme. Same men. Same outcome variable. A conclusion that flips sign.

Which Way Does the Bias Run?

The intuition students arrive with: "people who volunteer for a programme are motivated, so selection bias makes programmes look better than they are."

If that were the whole story, the general‑population comparison would make this programme look better than the experiment does. It came out about $15,000 below zero, against the experiment's +$1,794.

What would have to be true of the two groups for that to happen? Vote on the next slide.

Poll: The Sign Flip

The randomised experiment says the training programme raised earnings by +$1,794. Compared against a general‑population group instead, the same programme looks like it cut earnings by roughly $15,000.

Why?

A) The programme really did harm some participants
B) The comparison group was better off to begin with, so the bias runs downward
C) The experimental sample is too small to trust
D) Earnings are too skewed for a difference in means to mean anything

Answer: B — The Bias Runs Downward

The men who enrolled were much more disadvantaged than a general‑population sample. They would have earned less even with no training at all — so the second term in the decomposition is large and negative and swamps a real, positive effect.

Why A is tempting but wrong: the randomised estimate is the one that identifies harm or benefit, and it is positive. The negative number is a property of the comparison group, not of the programme.

Selection bias has no default sign. Reason about the comparison you made.

What Random Assignment Gives You

$$D_i \perp\!\!\!\perp \big(Y_i(1),\, Y_i(0)\big)$$

Interpretation: knowing who got treated tells you nothing about what either outcome would have been. That is a claim about the assignment mechanism — not about the data you collected.

$$E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] = E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0]$$

Interpretation: in expectation the two groups had identical baselines — so the selection‑bias term is exactly zero.

The Estimator, and Why It Seems Too Simple

$$\hat{\tau} = \bar{Y}_{\text{treatment}} - \bar{Y}_{\text{control}}$$

Interpretation: subtract two sample means. Under random assignment this is an unbiased estimator of the ATE.

Chapter 8's t‑test computed this exact quantity and called it a statistical difference.

Today we compute it again and call it a causal effect. Nothing in the arithmetic changed.

What lets us say "caused" is the random assignment, made before any data existed.

2  •  Analysing the Experiment

Permutation for the p‑value. Bootstrap for the size. Power before either.

Why Last Week's Tool Is Uncomfortable Here

The t‑test leans on the Central Limit Theorem: it needs the sampling distribution of the mean to be roughly normal.

Tech metrics break that. Revenue per user, session length, transaction value — heavily right‑skewed and zero‑inflated. Most users spend nothing; a few spend enormously.

So does our dataset. Roughly half the LaLonde 1978 earnings are exactly zero. That distribution is nowhere near a bell curve.

Chapter 7 warned that CLT convergence is slow for heavy tails. We need inference that does not ask the data to be anything in particular.

The Permutation Test: One Idea

If the treatment did nothing, then the words "treatment" and "control" are just labels stuck on rows — and shuffling them should produce differences that look like the one you observed.

Think like an economist. This is Fisher's sharp null: if \(Y_i(1) = Y_i(0)\) for every single \(i\), then every outcome is fixed and the only thing that varies across the universe of possible experiments is the label. That is the potential‑outcomes framework turned directly into an algorithm.

It needs no normality, no equal variances and no t‑distribution: the null distribution is built from your own data.

Poll: Reading the Interval

The bootstrap 95% CI for the LaLonde ATE is [$575, $3,262].

Which statement does that interval support?

A) 95% of trainees earned between $575 and $3,262 more
B) The true effect is $1,794 with 95% probability
C) The data are consistent with an average effect anywhere from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars
D) The effect is not statistically distinguishable from zero

Answer: C — It Is About the Average

The interval is a statement about \(\tau = E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]\). Individual effects \(\tau_i\) remain unobservable — that was the fundamental problem, twenty minutes ago.

Why A is tempting but wrong: it turns an interval for one number — the mean effect — into a range covering 445 different people. D is simply false: the interval excludes zero, which is the same message as p = 0.0046.

Power: The Decision You Make Before Any Data Exists

$$n = \frac{(z_{\alpha/2} + z_{\beta})^2 \,(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_0^2)}{\delta^2}$$

\(z_{\alpha/2} = 1.96\) at \(\alpha = 0.05\)

\(z_{\beta} = 0.84\) for 80% power

\(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_0^2\) — how noisy the metric is

\(\delta\) — the Minimum Detectable Effect: the smallest change you would act on

Interpretation: the MDE sits in the denominator, squared. That single fact is most of what this formula has to teach you.

Halve the Effect, Quadruple the Users

Because \(\delta\) is squared, detecting an effect half as large takes four times as many users.

Chapter example: a 2‑percentage‑point lift on a 10% conversion baseline needs about 3,500 users per group — 7,000 total, a week at a thousand visitors a day. Shrink the target to 1 point and you need roughly 14,000 per group.

The useful question is whether an effect this small is worth the traffic it takes to detect.

3  •  How a Correct Experiment Can Still Mislead

Randomisation was necessary. Four reasons it was not sufficient.

Failure 1: Peeking

The experiment is planned for seven days. On day three the dashboard shows p = 0.03. The PM asks whether you can ship now.

So let us measure the error rate. The lab runs 1,000 A/A tests — both arms drawn from the same 10% conversion distribution, no true effect anywhere — and peeks at n = 50, 100, 200, 500.

Four Peeks, Three Times the Error Rate

Poll: Day Three

Your A/B test is planned for seven days. On day three the primary metric hits p = 0.03.

What is the defensible move?

A) Ship it — significant is significant
B) Keep running to the sample size you committed to
C) Extend the test until p drops below 0.01
D) Ship it, but keep monitoring the metric afterwards

Answer: B — Finish the Test You Designed

A and D are the same decision rule — both stop on a threshold crossing, which is exactly the procedure that produced 15.6%. "Monitoring afterwards" does not undo a stopping rule that has already been applied.

Why C is worse: it is peeking with an asymmetric stopping rule — you keep going only when the answer displeases you. The legitimate escape is a sequential design chosen in advance, with always‑valid p‑values built for continuous monitoring.

The Result That Surprises Everyone

You would expect industry A/B testing to be riddled with p‑hacking — 20 metrics, report the one that crossed.

Miller & Hosanagar (2025) analysed 2,270 experiments across 242 firms on a major e‑commerce platform and found essentially no evidence of systematic p‑hacking.

Why? Unlike academic publishing, shipping a false positive does not pay. The feature launches, the revenue does not arrive, and the market tells you within a quarter. The incentive runs the opposite way from academic publishing.

And Yet One in Five Is Still Wrong

Berman & Van den Bulte (2022): 18–25% of "significant" A/B results are false positives — with no manipulation at all.

The cause is the base rate, not the analyst. About 70% of tested interventions are true nulls. Do the arithmetic on 100 features:

70

truly null → \(\approx\) 3.5 cross 0.05 by luck

30

truly real → \(\approx\) 24 detected at 80% power

13%

of your "wins" are noise — and it climbs fast as power falls

This is Chapter 5's base‑rate fallacy in a product review. Fix: Benjamini‑Hochberg across metrics, or a hierarchical rule — primary first, secondaries only if the primary moves.

Failure 2: You Optimised the Wrong Thing

Goodhart's Law (Chapter 2): when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Meta gamed DAU/MAU with notification spam; Wells Fargo gamed cross‑selling with unauthorised accounts.

A recommendation engine test raises click‑through by 40%. Ship it?

Not if it got those clicks by pushing low‑quality clickbait. Long‑run satisfaction and retention fell sharply. The experiment was correctly randomised, correctly analysed, and correctly measured the wrong quantity.

No statistical fix exists for this. The defence is in the metric design, before launch.

The Defence: A Metric Hierarchy

TierThe question it answersExample
PrimaryDid the one thing we set out to move, move?conversion rate
GuardrailDid anything we depend on break?page load time, error rate, support tickets
Counter‑metricWhat did the primary gain cost us?average order value, refund rate

Decision rule: ship only if the primary improves and every guardrail shows non‑inferiority — no statistically significant degradation.

Economist at Work: Wayfair's Demeter

The bind. Boston‑based Wayfair's true impact on customer lifetime value takes 60–90 days to appear. Waiting two months per test is far too slow — but optimising on three‑day clicks runs into Goodhart's Law.

Surrogate indices. Short‑run signals from days 3–7 — add‑to‑cart velocity, initial session duration — are used to forecast the 60‑day revenue effect. Internally, Demeter recovered an accurate estimate of the full 60‑day lift by day 14.

Geo‑experiments. For direct mail and vendor networks, user‑level randomisation is impossible. Wayfair partitions the country into 210 mutually exclusive geographic units, assigns them by integer optimisation, and estimates lift with synthetic difference‑in‑differences.

Remember those 210 clusters: they come back in ten minutes to solve a different problem.

Failure 3: Sample Ratio Mismatch

You designed a 50/50 split. You observe 50.5 / 49.5 on a million users. Noise, or a bug?

On a million users, a chi‑squared test on that split is enormous. It is a bug essentially every time. Fabijan et al. (2019) find SRM in about 6% of experiments at Microsoft and Booking.com.

Usual causes: logging that drops treatment rows, bots clustering in one arm, a slower redirect on one variant so users abandon before being logged, trigger conditions that interact with treatment.

What to do: halt, diagnose, rerun. Microsoft's platform blocks the display of p‑values until the pipeline is fixed. No adjustment repairs a broken randomisation.

Failure 4: The Assumption Nobody States

$$Y_i(D_1, D_2, \ldots, D_n) = Y_i(D_i) \quad \text{for all } i$$

Interpretation: your outcome depends on your own assignment and nobody else's. This is SUTVA — the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption.

Everything in Sections 1 and 2 assumed it without saying so. And it fails on any platform where users compete for the same finite thing — listings, riders, attention.

Three Ways Users Interfere

Two‑sided marketplaces. Treated Airbnb users with discounts book the listings control users would have booked. The control group's outcomes get worse because the treatment worked.

Social networks. A treated user's new sharing feature puts more content in front of their friends — who are in the control group. The control group is indirectly treated.

Resource constraints. Faster treated drivers consume dispatch requests from a fixed pool of riders, suppressing control drivers' performance.

In all three the measured gap is real — and partly created by the experiment itself.

How Much of the Effect Was Interference?

19.76%

of the estimate, at least, was interference bias

Holtz et al. (2025) ran a meta‑experiment on Airbnb: they randomised the randomisation scheme itself, comparing individual‑level assignment against cluster assignment, and measured the gap.

A correctly executed, correctly analysed A/B test overstated its own effect by a fifth — and the true figure is a lower bound.

Holtz, D. et al. (2025), Management Science 71(1), 390–406.

The Fix Is the Unit, Not the Sample Size

Cluster randomisation

Randomise whole geographies, not users. Wayfair's 210 units — the same construction, now doing identification work.

Switchback designs

Alternate treatment and control across time periods instead of across users. Standard for ride‑hailing marketplaces.

Graph clustering

Partition the social graph into loosely connected components and randomise those, so spillover stays inside an arm.

The lesson of Section 3: the unit of randomisation matters as much as the fact of randomisation. A bigger sample makes an interference‑biased estimate more precise, not more correct.

Bojinov & Simchi‑Levi (2020) on switchback designs; Chapter 9 §6.4.

Poll: The +12% Driver

A ride‑hailing platform randomises drivers into a faster dispatch algorithm. Assignment is clean, the split is exactly 50/50, the analysis is correct. Treated drivers complete 12% more rides.

What is most likely wrong with that number?

A) Sample ratio mismatch
B) It is inflated — treated drivers took rides away from control drivers
C) Nothing — it was randomised, so it is causal
D) The metric should have been revenue, not rides

Answer: B — SUTVA Fails

The pool of riders is fixed. A control driver's outcome now depends on the treatment group's assignment, so \(Y_i(D_1,\ldots,D_n) \neq Y_i(D_i)\). The measured gap contains a real gain plus a transfer.

Why C is tempting but wrong: randomisation removes selection bias. It does nothing about interference bias. Those are different failures, and random assignment solves only one of them. The repair is a switchback or geo‑cluster design — not a larger sample.

Key Takeaways

Randomisation, not adjustment. The formula is the t‑test's. Random assignment is what lets you say "caused".

Permutation for the p, bootstrap for the size. Neither asks the earnings distribution to be anything at all.

Power is a design decision. Halve the MDE, quadruple the users. Decide before the data exists.

A correct experiment can still be wrong. Peeking, Goodhart, SRM, interference — none is fixed by more data.

Significance is not a decision. Cookie Cats is close to significant, looks large by any naive standard, and is still not a clear case to ship.
