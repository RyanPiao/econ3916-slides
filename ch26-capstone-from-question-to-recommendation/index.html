1  •  Prediction or Causation?

The question you must answer before you write a line of code.

Umbrella Problems and Rain Dance Problems

Kleinberg, Ludwig, Mullainathan & Obermeyer (2015) named the split.

Umbrella — prediction

You do not care why it rains. You care whether to carry an umbrella tomorrow. A forecast is the whole deliverable.

Rain dance — causation

You must prove the dance causes rain, not merely that dances and rain co-occur. A forecast is worthless here.

The decision rule. Ask what the stakeholder will do with the answer. Whom to target → prediction. Whether to intervene → causation.

Two Questions, Two of Everything

Prediction — “umbrella”Causation — “rain dance”
GoalForecast $\hat{y}$ as accurately as possibleEstimate the effect $\tau$ of $T$ on $Y$
MethodRandom Forest, LASSO, logistic (Ch 16–19)RCT, DAG adjustment, DML (Ch 9, 10, 24)
FeaturesEverything predictiveOnly exogenous controls — endogeneity biases it
MetricRMSE, AUC, expected cost (Ch 18)CI for $\hat\tau$, balance checks, placebo tests
Sentence“Predicts X with Y% accuracy”“Caused a Z-unit change (95% CI: [a, b])”

Every row differs. Getting the first question wrong makes every later choice wrong too.

One Dataset, Two Questions

UCI Bank Marketing — Portuguese bank telemarketing, binary outcome: did the customer subscribe to a term deposit? About 11% did. The CEO asks two things.

“Which customers should we call next?”

Umbrella. You do not care why anyone subscribes. Fit a Random Forest, use every pre-call feature, rank by predicted probability.

Deliverable: a call list.

“Does calling cause them to subscribe?”

Rain dance. Would they have subscribed anyway? You need randomisation, or a defensible DAG, or DML.

Deliverable: an effect with an interval.

Same 45,211 rows. Completely different methods, because the decisions are different.

The Timing Test: duration

Call length in seconds is enormously predictive — long calls end in subscriptions. It is also endogenous: the call is long because the customer was interested.

And for targeting it is not even usable. You learn the call length after making the call you were trying to decide about.

The test: when does this value become known, relative to the decision I am modelling? Not “does this sound like it leaks.”

Apply it to poutcome. It sounds like an outcome. It is the result of the previous campaign, already on file before the phone rings — so it is fair game, and in Lab 26 it is the single most important feature (MDI 0.167).

Poll: What Kind of Question Is This?

A hospital administrator asks: “Which patients discharged today are most likely to be readmitted within 30 days, so we can schedule follow-up calls?”

A) Causal — we need to know whether follow-up calls reduce readmission
B) Prediction — we need to rank patients by readmission risk
C) Causal — readmission is an outcome, so this is a treatment-effect question
D) Neither — this needs a randomised trial before any model is appropriate

Answer: B — Prediction

The decision is whom to call. She is not asking whether calls work — she has already decided to make them and needs to know whom to spend them on.

Why C is tempting but wrong: the presence of an outcome variable does not make a question causal. What makes it causal is asking whether an intervention moved it. Note the version that would be causal: “do our follow-up calls actually reduce readmission?” — same data, different question, different method entirely.

2  •  A Model Is Worthless Until You Compare It

You already produced the proof. In Lab 17.

Your Own 90.4%

Lab 17, credit-default data at 9.6% prevalence. Your tuned classifier: 90.4%. A model with no features at all: 90.4%.

Every hour of feature engineering added exactly zero percentage points over a model that never looked at the data.

Accuracy is not the problem. An accuracy number is uninterpretable without its baseline — and the baseline is a property of your data, not of your model.

Poll: Is This Model Good?

A classmate reports: “My fraud model gets 99.2% accuracy on the test set.”

Fraud occurs in 0.7% of transactions.

A) Excellent — 99.2% is near-perfect
B) Cannot tell — and the baseline here is 99.3%
C) Bad — accuracy is never a valid metric for any problem
D) Cannot tell — you need a bigger test set

Answer: B — Below the Baseline

Predicting “not fraud” for every transaction is right 99.3% of the time. The model is below the no-skill baseline. Ask for recall, precision, or expected cost.

Why C is tempting but wrong: accuracy is a valid metric, but it is meaningless without its baseline. “Never use accuracy” swaps one thoughtless rule for another. Why D is wrong: more data would tighten your estimate of a number that is already telling you the wrong thing.

3  •  The Threshold Is the Decision

Your model outputs a probability. You output the decision.

What Lab 18 Measured

A missed positive costs ten times a false alarm. You swept the threshold.

$t = 0.068$ optimal0.4587
$t = 0.5$ the default0.9437

Accepting the default roughly doubles the cost of running the model — identical model, identical probabilities, decision rule chosen by nobody.

Poll: Where Does the Threshold Go?

Your model flags equipment likely to fail. A missed failure costs \$50,000. A needless inspection costs \$500. You are classifying at the default 0.5.

A) Raise it above 0.5 — be more certain before acting
B) Lower it well below 0.5 — accept false alarms to avoid missed failures
C) Keep 0.5 — it is the neutral, unbiased choice
D) It does not matter once the model is accurate enough

Answer: B — Far Below 0.5

At a 100:1 cost ratio you should be willing to inspect a great deal of healthy equipment to avoid one missed failure.

Why C is tempting but wrong: 0.5 feels neutral because it is symmetric — but symmetry in the probability is only neutral if the two errors cost the same. Here one costs a hundred times the other, so the default encodes an assumption about your business that is simply false. Why D is wrong: a more accurate model at the wrong threshold can cost more than a worse model at the right one — which is exactly what you found in Lab 18 when you swept the cost ratio.

What This Means for the Dashboard You Are About to Build

The Final Project asks for “an input control that changes a prediction” and “an honest display of uncertainty.”

Threshold is the natural control. Let the user move it and watch the expected cost, the number flagged, and the recall move with it.

That dashboard shows the stakeholder their decision, not your model. It is the difference between a demo and a tool — and it is 14 rubric points.

4  •  Prediction Does Not License a Causal Claim

And the honest sentence that says so without hedging.

The Most Misread Chart in Applied ML

Every one of these you put in front of a stakeholder needs the banner.

What the VP Will Say Without Your Help

“Employment levels drive term deposit subscriptions. Let's wait until employment rises, then launch the campaign.”

That is a causal recommendation extracted from a predictive ranking, and it is wrong in a specific way.

nr.employed moves the bank's marketing calendar and customers' willingness to save, independently. It is a confounder.

Nobody at the bank can change the national employment level. It is not a lever they can pull.

Recommending that they wait for it is recommending that they do nothing, dressed up as analysis.

The Honest Sentence

“X is the strongest predictor of Y. This is predictive importance, not a causal effect — and here is what it would take to know more: [the design].”

The third clause is what separates a careful analyst from a hedging one. “This is not causal” alone sounds like a disclaimer added to avoid blame. Naming the design turns the same sentence into a proposal.

“To know whether calling causes subscription, we would randomise who gets called in the next campaign and compare — the Ch 9 A/B test, on 5% of the list.”

“If we cannot randomise, we need every confounder measured and a defensible DAG, or DML with the macro indicators as controls (Ch 24) — and I would want it to agree with an experiment before I trusted it.”

Discussion: Write the Honest Sentence

Take the top feature from your own Final Project model — or nr.employed if yours is not running yet.

1. The claim

X is the strongest predictor of Y.

2. The limit

Why this is predictive and not causal, in terms of your data.

3. The design

What experiment or strategy would let you say more — and what it costs.

Poll: Which Sentence Would You Sign?

A) “Employment levels are the biggest driver of subscriptions, so we should time campaigns to the labour market.”
B) “nr.employed is our strongest predictor; it is a macro confounder we cannot act on, so the actionable signal is in the client-level features below it.”
C) “Our model achieves 92% accuracy, so its feature ranking is reliable.”
D) “Feature importance is unreliable, so we should not report it at all.”

Answer: B — State It, Limit It, Redirect

B states the finding, names why it does not license action, and points at the part of the ranking that is actionable. All three steps in one sentence.

Why D is tempting but wrong: hiding the chart avoids the question — the ranking is real information about where the predictive signal lives. Label it and use it. Why C is wrong: accuracy is a statement about predictions, not about whether a ranking carries causal meaning. A perfectly accurate model can rank a confounder first.

5  •  From Result to Recommendation

Start with the answer. Everything else is appendix.

Invert the Term Paper

Situation — a baseline everyone accepts. “We contact 50,000 customers a month at 11% conversion.”

Complication — the anomaly you found. “Our targeting is effectively random; the top decile converts at about 23%.”

Resolution — the action. “Use model-based targeting next month. Expected uplift: about 6,000 subscriptions a month.”

You build S → C → R. You deliver R first. Both orders are correct.

The Minimum Viable Deliverable

One visual

readable in under ten seconds

One claim

with clear uncertainty bounds

One recommendation

what to do, given the claim

Everything else is appendix. Adding your robustness checks to the main deck weakens the argument: it triggers the paradox of choice, and a decision-maker facing too many numbers defers rather than decides.

Reporting Uncertainty

Spiegelhalter (2017). Standalone percentages are misread. Use natural frequencies: not “a 15% chance of recession” but “out of 100 forecasts with this much uncertainty, about 15 end in recession.”

Fischhoff (2014). “A serious possibility” is read as anywhere from 20% to 80%. The IPCC fixed this by calibrating: very likely = 90–100%, likely = 66–100%.

1. Never a point estimate without bounds. Not “23% conversion” but “23% (95% CI: 19–27%).”

2. Translate the interval into English. “Our best estimate is about \$0.90, but we cannot rule out anything from a slight decrease to a \$2.10 increase.”

3. Say what you would need to know more. “To halve that interval we would need roughly 5,000 participants.”

Rule 3 is the most credibility-building sentence available to you, and it costs nothing.

Economist at Work: Stripe Capital

Stripe's lending arm had a textbook endogeneity problem: the variables that make a business eligible for a loan — revenue, longevity, credit — also predict success without it. Comparing funded to unfunded businesses measures selection, not lending.

Five Takeaways

Ask the question before you pick the method. Whom to target is prediction. Whether to intervene is causation.

An accuracy number without its baseline is not a result. Lab 17: 90.4% against a 90.4% no-skill baseline. Report the gap, never the level alone.

The threshold is the decision. Lab 18: cost 0.4587 at $t = 0.068$ against 0.9437 at the default. The default was chosen by someone who never saw your costs.

Say the honest sentence. “X is the strongest predictor of Y; this is predictive importance, not a causal effect” — then name the design that would let you say more.

Lead with the answer. One visual, one claim with bounds, one recommendation. California Housing, FRED, Lalonde and FedSpeak taught you the tools — they are only as useful as your ability to choose one and defend it.
