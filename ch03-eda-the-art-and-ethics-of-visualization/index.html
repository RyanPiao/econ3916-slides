Why We Visualize

Four datasets that agree on every number and on nothing else

Poll: Same Statistics, Same Data?

Two datasets have the same mean, the same standard deviation and the same correlation. How different can they be?

A) Essentially identical — those three pin the data down
B) Similar in shape, different only in a few outliers
C) Completely different — one can be a curve and the other a straight line
D) Impossible to say without the sample size

Answer: C — Completely Different

The same mean, SD and correlation can hide completely different data. Plot first.

Those three statistics describe center, spread and linear association. That is all. They are blind to shape, curvature, clusters and leverage — and a dataset is mostly shape.

The Same Four Datasets, Plotted

I — the textbook case

A linear cloud. The fitted line is the right model and the correlation means what you think it means.

II — a parabola

Deterministic curvature. \(r = 0.816\) and the linear model is simply the wrong functional form.

III — one outlier

Ten points on a perfect line, one off it. That single point tilts the slope away from the pattern the other ten agree on.

IV — one point does all the work

Every x is 8 except one. Delete that observation and the slope is undefined. The fit rests on n = 1.

Three of the four would send you to a different chapter of this course.

Section 2

Tufte: Two Numbers for a Chart

Is it efficient? Is it honest? Both have formulas.

The Data-Ink Ratio

Data-Ink Ratio = ink that shows data ÷ all the ink. Erase ink that encodes no number.
$$\text{Data-Ink Ratio} = \frac{\text{Data Ink}}{\text{Total Ink Used in Graphic}}$$

In plain language: of all the ink on the page, what fraction is doing work?

Runs from 0 (all decoration) to 1 (all data). Tufte's prescription is subtractive: erase non-data ink, then erase redundant data ink.

Tufte · data-ink · chartjunk

Five Numbers, Twice

Both panels carry the same five values and both start at zero. Only the ink differs.

The Lie Factor

Lie Factor = size of the effect shown ÷ size of the effect in the data. Honest is about 1.
$$\text{Lie Factor} = \frac{\text{Size of effect shown in the graphic}}{\text{Size of effect in the data}}$$

In plain language: how many times bigger is the picture than the fact?

1.0

honest

> 1.5

meaningful exaggeration

> 5.0

serious distortion

< 1.0

understating — also a distortion

Tufte's own tolerance is 0.95–1.05. The bands above are this course's rules of thumb, not his.

Poll: What Is the Lie Factor?

Revenue goes from $98M to $102M. The truncated chart makes Q4 look about three times Q1. What is the Lie Factor?

A) About 3 — the bar is three times as tall
B) About 4 — revenue rose about 4%
C) About 49
D) About 200 — the bar grew 200%

Answer: C — About 49

Lie Factor ≈ 49: the bars grow 200%, revenue grows 4.1%. 2.00 ÷ 0.041.

The Lie Factor is a ratio of two changes, not a ratio of two heights. A said “three times as tall” and stopped; D computed the visual change and never divided by the real one.

Section 3

Seven Ways a Chart Lies

Name the mechanism, not the feeling — and learn the check for each

Three That Distort the Geometry

Three lies bend the geometry: a truncated axis, dual y-axes, and area encoding.

1. Truncated axis

The baseline moves off zero and small differences fill the frame.

Check: read the bottom tick before you read the bars.

2. Dual y-axes

Two series, two independent scales, and the author chooses both. Any correlation can be manufactured or erased.

Check: two axes in different units with no stated rule for aligning them.

3. Area and volume encoding

Double the radius and the area quadruples; in three dimensions the volume goes up eightfold. Bubble charts are the usual offender.

Check: ask whether the value is the radius or the area. It is almost never stated.

Why Bubbles Betray You

Cleveland & McGill (1984) ranked visual encodings by how accurately people read them. Position beats length; length beats area; area beats color. The ranking is empirical, not aesthetic.

Three That Distort the Frame

Three lies bend the frame: a cherry-picked window, a mislabelled metric, missing context.

4. Cherry-picked time window

Start the series after the crash and the crash is gone. The window is a framing decision and it is rarely disclosed.

Check: ask what happened immediately before the first data point.

5. Metric mislabeling

The geometry is fine. The axis label describes a different variable than the one plotted.

Check: compare the axis name with the variable's actual definition.

6. Missing context

No uncertainty band implies a precision the data may not support. A spike with no note that last month was revised is not false, and it is not honest either.

Check: ask what is not on the chart.

A Chart With Perfect Geometry

A Financial Times chart on AI hiring labelled its line “hires”. It was widely shared.

The line dropped below zero.

Which is impossible. You cannot hire a negative number of people.

Poll: Which Mechanism Broke It?

A line labelled “Hires per month” goes below zero. The underlying series is net employment change. Which mechanism is this?

A) Truncated axis — the y-axis extends below zero
B) Metric mislabeling — a net flow labelled as a gross flow
C) Cherry-picked window — the boom before it was cropped out
D) Missing context — there are no uncertainty bands

Answer: B — The Label Lied

The label lied: a net change — hires minus departures — was labelled 'hires'.

Every mark on that chart was in the right place. The variable was not the one named on the axis. A line that goes below zero is a net change — hires minus departures — wearing a gross-flow label. Geometry checks would never have caught it; reading the data dictionary would have caught it in ten seconds.

The Seventh: Reordering

Reordering: the categories are sorted to tell a story instead of shown in their real order.

May 2020. The Georgia Department of Public Health published a bar chart of confirmed COVID-19 cases in the state's five worst-affected counties. It appeared to show a steady decline.

The dates along the x-axis were not in chronological order. They had been arranged so the bars descended.

Put the dates back in order and the decline disappears. The state acknowledged the error and redesigned the chart.

Source: Georgia Department of Public Health COVID-19 dashboard, May 2020, and the state's subsequent correction.

Think Like an Economist

Every chart makes three decisions. Interrogate all three — including when you drew it.

What to show

Which variables? Which time window? Which units? Levels or changes?

How to encode

Position, length, area, color? Linear or log? One axis or two?

What to omit

Missing data? Uncertainty bands? Context variables? Revisions?

The chart is not a window into the data. It is an argument.

One Dataset, Four Arguments

Panels A and B

A — honest

Full range, y from zero. The 1973 peak, the 1995 trough and the 2020 break are all visible at once. This is the Chapter 2 chart.

B — dishonest

y truncated to $19–$26. The same series now looks violently volatile. Month-to-month wobble that was invisible in A dominates the frame.

Who wants it: anyone arguing that wages move a lot — in either direction, depending on which end of the chart they point at.

Panels C and D

C — dishonest by omission

Window cropped to 2015–2026. A gentle rise with one 2020 spike, and forty years of decline-and-recovery gone. y still starts at zero, so nothing about the geometry is wrong.

Could be honest — if the window choice were disclosed and justified.

D — depends on the question

Log scale, full range. Right when proportional change is the question or the data spans orders of magnitude. This series spans a factor of about 1.3, so the log scale mostly flattens the structural breaks that are the interesting part.

When Is Truncation Acceptable?

Truncation misleads only when it makes a small change look large. Lie Factor above 2: annotate.

Not automatically dishonest. Body temperature in Fahrenheit lives in 97–104. A policy rate lives in 0–10%. Starting at zero would spend most of the chart on a region with no data.

The test. Does the truncation make a small change look large? If the change is really large within the relevant range, truncating is informative. If it is small and the chart makes it look dramatic, the chart is misleading.

The practical rule. If the Lie Factor is above 2.0, annotate the chart: “Note: y-axis does not start at zero.”

Disclosure is the floor, not the ceiling.

Common Misconception

On a log scale equal distances are equal ratios. Use it when data span orders of magnitude.

“Log scales are dishonest.”

For variables spanning orders of magnitude — stock indices, city populations — log is more honest than linear, and constant growth becomes a straight line. For a series in a narrow range, linear carries more information.

The choice follows the question, not a rule.

The EDA Workflow

You cannot redesign a chart that does not exist yet

Four Steps, In Order

EDA runs in order: structure, distributions, relationships, anomalies — before every model.

Tukey (1977) called it detective work: pursue clues, form hypotheses, let formal analysis test them later.

Step Question Tools
1. StructureHow many rows, columns, types? Is it tidy?df.shape, df.dtypes, df.head()
2. DistributionsNormal, skewed, bimodal? Long tail?df.hist(), sns.kdeplot()
3. RelationshipsWhat correlates with what? Any curvature?sns.pairplot(), df.corr()
4. AnomaliesOutliers? Impossible values? Duplicates? Holes?df.describe(), box plots, df.duplicated()

Run this before every model in this course — OLS in Chapter 12, random forests in Chapter 19.

EDA = exploratory data analysis · Tukey

Step 1: What the File Is Made Of

Two Views of One Variable

Histogram

Bins the range and counts. Concrete, intuitive, good at skew, gaps and multiple peaks.

Its tuning parameter: bin width. Too few bins loses detail; too many and noise wins.

Kernel density estimate

Places a small Gaussian at every observation and sums them. A smooth estimate of the density.

Its tuning parameter: bandwidth. Narrow is noisy; wide can smooth two modes into one.

Plot both. The histogram gives you concrete counts, the KDE gives you the shape. Neither setting has a universally correct value — they are design decisions, exactly like an axis range.

Three Bandwidths, Three Claims

Choosing the Bin Width

Sturges — the classic rule (R's default)

$$\text{bins} = \lceil \log_2(n) + 1 \rceil$$

Built for roughly normal data. It gives too few bins for skewed economic data, which is most economic data.

Freedman–Diaconis — the better default here

$$\text{width} = 2 \times \text{IQR} \times n^{-1/3}$$

In plain language: make the bins wider when the data is spread out, and narrower when you have more of it. In seaborn: sns.histplot(data, bins="fd").

Suspect two modes and the KDE looks unimodal? Try bw_adjust=0.5.

What the Software Actually Does

Anomalies: The Holes Have Structure

Missing because the outcome was bad, like war years, is MNAR. A country that did not exist is not missing.

Chapter 1's MCAR / MAR / MNAR is an EDA question, not a theory question. Pivot the panel to countries × years and look at where the holes are.

Anomalies: Values That Cannot Be

Beyond outliers, EDA catches logically impossible values — entries that violate domain constraints rather than statistical ones.

Negative GDP

a data entry error or a pipeline artifact

Age 150

an encoding error, usually a sentinel value

A percentage of 347

a unit confusion, or not a percentage at all

These hide from summary statistics. 100,000 clean rows and 50 negative-GDP rows barely move the mean — but a model trained on them learns that negative GDP is possible. Run df.describe() and read the min and max rows against your domain knowledge. It takes seconds.
