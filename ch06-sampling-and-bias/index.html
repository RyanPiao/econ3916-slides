What Gives the Guarantee

Probability sampling, and the three designs you will actually meet

What Makes a Sample a “Probability Sample”

Every member of the population has a known, non-zero chance of being selected.

That single property is what makes unbiased estimation possible. Note what it is not about:

 It is not about the sample being big.

 It is not about the sample feeling random or arbitrary.

 It is about the selection probabilities being quantities you know.

Simple Random Sampling, and What “Unbiased” Means

Every unit has the same chance $\pi_i = n/N$ of being picked. Then:

$$E[\bar{y}] = \bar{Y}$$

$\bar{y}$ — the mean you compute from your $n$ sampled units

$\bar{Y}$ — the true mean over all $N$ units in the population

$E[\cdot]$ — “the average value of, over many repeats”

In English: any one sample will miss. But repeat the whole exercise over and over and the sample means centre exactly on the truth — no systematic push in either direction. That is all “unbiased” means, and it is a property of the procedure, not of the one dataset on your laptop.

Two Designs That Beat Plain Random

Stratified sampling — sort the population into groups, then sample randomly inside each group.

Like sorting a bag of mixed candy by colour and grabbing a random handful of each. Every flavour is guaranteed to show up.

Cluster sampling — sample whole groups at once, all-or-nothing.

Cheaper when the population is spread across a map. Riskier: neighbours resemble each other, so a cluster carries less information than the same count of scattered individuals.

Why stratified usually wins. Plain random sampling represents every subgroup in expectation. Stratified represents it in this particular sample. When your sample is small or a subgroup is rare, that gap matters most.

The Three Designs, Side by Side

Same population, same number of units selected. Three different guarantees.

Poll: Which Ingredient Shrinks?

An estimate’s error has three ingredients: how bent the selection is, how variable the outcome is, and how much of the population you missed. You collect ten times more data from the same source. Which shrinks?

A) All three
B) How bent the selection is
C) How much you missed — and only that one
D) None — the error is fixed

Answer: C — Only the Third

More data from the same pipe closes the “how much did I miss” gap and touches nothing else. How bent the selection is is a property of the mechanism; how variable the outcome is is a property of the world. Neither knows or cares how many rows you have.

Why A is tempting but wrong: “more data helps” is true, and it matters everywhere else in this course. Here it reaches exactly one term of three, and this lecture is about the other two.

One Condition, Four Costumes

Selection, survivorship, nonresponse, self-selection — and the single line underneath all four

The Condition Underneath Everything

Selection bias means the chance of showing up in your data is correlated with the thing you are trying to measure:

$$\text{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0$$

In English: if being in the dataset and what the answer would have been travel together at all, your sample mean sits off the population mean — and it stays off no matter how many rows you add.

Meng’s Identity: Error as a Product of Three

$$\bar{y}_n - \bar{Y}_N \;=\; \rho_{R,Y} \;\times\; \sigma_Y \;\times\; \sqrt{\frac{N-n}{n}}$$

$\rho_{R,Y}$

how bent the selection mechanism is

More data: no help.
Property of the mechanism.

$\sigma_Y$

how spread out the outcome is

More data: no help.
Property of the world.

$\sqrt{(N-n)/n}$

how much of the population you missed

More data: yes.
The only one.

In English: your error is a product. Random sampling drives the first term to zero, the whole product collapses to zero, and the third term does all the work — that is the happy Topic 5 story. If the first term is not zero, shrinking only the third factor cannot save you.

The Big Data Paradox

For a fixed sample size, the error scales with $\sqrt{N/n}$ — with the size of the population, not just the sample.

So the bigger the world you are trying to describe, the more a small bias costs you. A bent mechanism that is harmless when you are describing a village is catastrophic when you are describing a country.

In the age of big data, quality matters far more than quantity.

This is the result behind the hook: 2.3 million responses reduced to the worth of 400, by a correlation of −0.005, because $N$ was the whole American electorate.

Costume 1: Survivorship Bias

Backtest a momentum strategy on today’s Nasdaq 100 members and it looks brilliant. Put the dot-com casualties back in and returns more than halve while maximum drawdowns roughly double.

A 2024 study found that ignoring survivorship overstated median mutual fund alpha by 0.60% a year.

Think like an economist. Before accepting any performance number — fund returns, startup success rates, hospital quality scores, graduation rates — ask: who is missing, and did they leave because of the outcome? If yes, that is $\text{Cov}(R,Y) \neq 0$, and it is Chapter 1’s MNAR wearing a finance suit.

Who Is Left in the 3%?

Topic 1’s Framework, Doing Real Work

Term The nonresponse version Can you fix it?
MCAR Who answers is pure chance Nothing to fix. You just have a smaller sample.
MAR Answering depends on things you can see — age, ZIP, gender Yes. Reweight the groups you can observe.
MNAR Answering depends on the answer itself Not fully. You would have to model why people stayed silent.

Common misconception: “just weight the sample to match the census.” Weighting fixes MAR. It cannot fix MNAR — the young people who did answer may not represent young people at all. Weighting corrects the group proportions and leaves the within-group distortion exactly where it was.

Four Costumes, One Condition

Selection

The frame excluded people whose answers differ.

Survivorship

They left, because of the outcome.

Nonresponse

They were asked and stayed silent, because of the outcome.

Self-selection

They walked in on their own, because of the outcome.

$$\text{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0$$

Different stories about how the switch got flipped. Identical damage.

Poll: Why Is Stability Bad News?

Across 100 runs the biased estimate’s errors have a standard deviation of 0.01 points; the random estimate’s is 0.20. The biased one is twenty times more stable. Why is that bad news?

A) It is good news — stable estimates are what we want
B) Stability only reflects the bigger sample; it says nothing about being right
C) The random sample must have a bug
D) Low variance always implies low bias

Answer: B — Precision and Accuracy Are Different Axes

The biased estimate is stable because 4.38 million observations pin down its own answer very tightly. Its answer is wrong. Worse: that tightness would show up as a narrow confidence interval — so the very mechanism producing the error also produces false reassurance about it.

Why A is tempting but wrong: a number that reproduces across reruns is exactly what people cite as evidence a finding is solid. Reproducibility tests your code. It cannot test your frame.

Your Split Is a Sampling Design

Training set

The sample your model learns from.

Test set

Stands in for the population your model will meet in deployment.

Titanic: One Keyword Argument

891 passengers, 342 survived — an overall rate of 0.384. Survival was wildly unequal: 63% in 1st class, 47% in 2nd, 24% in 3rd. Dropping rows with a missing Age leaves 714 passengers at a 0.406 rate.

Split Train rate Test rate Test deviation
train_test_split(X, y) 0.410 0.392 −1.46 pts
... , stratify=y) 0.406 0.406 −0.06 pts

One caveat. stratify=y balances survival, not passenger class. The stratified test set’s class shares came out closer to the population too — but that is a by-product, not a promise. If you need class balanced, stratify on it.

A 1.46-Point Hole, Closed to 0.06

It matters most when the class is rare: 2% fraud, 5% defaults, 1% click-through. A naive split can hand you a test set with almost no positive cases at all.

Data Leakage: Three Ways It Gets In

Leakage is information flowing from the test set into training. It is a survey where the pollster already knows the answers.

2. Feature leakage — using a variable that is a consequence of the outcome. Predicting loan default from total_payments: defaulted loans have fewer payments by definition.

3. Temporal leakage — training on 2020–2024 to predict 2019.

Why it belongs in this lecture: leakage breaks the independence of train and test. Topic 5 taught you that independence of draws is what makes the LLN work. Break it and the convergence guarantee dissolves — again.

What Leakage Looks Like in Numbers

Standardising Age on the Titanic data, two orders of operations:

Scale, then split −0.0253 0.9973 0.1009 1.0044
Split, then scale 0.0000 1.0000 0.1265 1.0072

The test set not being perfectly standardised is the correct result. It is unseen data with its own distribution. The fill values differ too: mean Age is 29.6991 computed from everything, 29.3325 from the training rows alone.

Poll: Find the Leak

You are predicting which customers churn next month. Which of these leaks?

A) months_as_customer, recorded at signup
B) Fitting StandardScaler on train, applying it to test
C) tickets_closed_after_cancellation
D) Splitting 80/20 with stratify=y on the churn label

Answer: C — Feature Leakage

That column can only be recorded after the outcome has already happened. It will look like a spectacular predictor right up to deployment, where the field is empty for every customer you actually need to score. B and D are the correct procedures; A is a legitimate feature, known at prediction time.

Why B is tempting but wrong: it is fitting the scaler on test data that leaks — not using a scaler at all. B describes exactly the fix.

Topic 6 in a Nutshell

Design, not size, decides whether an estimate is trustworthy. A probability sample gives you unbiasedness. A non-probability sample gives you nothing, at any scale.

One condition, four costumes. Selection, survivorship, nonresponse and self-selection are all $\text{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0$.

Error is a product of three terms — bent, spread, missed — and more data shrinks only the last one.

MCAR / MAR / MNAR is the diagnostic: no bias, correctable by weighting, or hard to fix.

In ML the training data IS the sample. The split is a sampling design, stratify=y is stratified sampling, and leakage is the violation of sample independence.
