A word that appears in many documents tells you nothing about what makes this meeting special.

A word that is frequent in this document but rare across the corpus — that is the distinctive signal.

This is exactly what TF-IDF formalizes.

Section 2

TF-IDF

Importance = Frequency × Rarity

Term Frequency – Inverse Document Frequency

\[\text{TF-IDF}(t, d, D) = \underbrace{\text{TF}(t, d)}_{\text{frequency in this doc}} \times \underbrace{\log\frac{|D|}{df(t)}}_{\text{rarity across corpus}}\]

TF(t, d)

How often word t appears in this document

|D|

Total documents in corpus (243 FOMC meetings in the lab)

df(t)

Documents containing word t (corpus-wide commonness)

High TF-IDF = frequent here, rare everywhere else. Low TF-IDF = ubiquitous word.

Worked Example: December 2008 FOMC

"recession"

TF = 2 / 4,641 = 0.00043

df = 87 of 243 meetings

IDF = log(243/87) ≈ 1.03

TF-IDF = 0.00044 ✓

"inflation"

TF = 28 / 4,641 = 0.0060

df = 243 of 243 meetings

IDF = log(243/243) = log(1) = 0

TF-IDF = 0 ✗

"inflation" appears 14× as often but carries zero information — information ∝ rarity, not frequency

In Code: TfidfVectorizer

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

vectorizer = TfidfVectorizer(
    max_features=1000,       # Keep top 1000 words
    stop_words='english',    # Remove common English words
    ngram_range=(1, 2),      # Bigrams: "interest rate", "labor market"
    sublinear_tf=True,       # 1 + log(tf) — diminishing returns
)

X_tfidf = vectorizer.fit_transform(documents)
# Shape: (243, 1000) — 243 meetings × 1000 features

sublinear_tf: Mentioning "growth" 20 times ≠ 20× more growth-focused. Diminishing marginal information.

Discussion Debrief

Problem 1: Document Length

15 mentions in 15,000 words (0.1%) < 12 mentions in 5,000 words (0.24%). TF normalizes for document length.

Problem 2: Corpus Context

"inflation" appears in all 243 sets of minutes (lab Step 3), so even 15 mentions is not distinctive. IDF penalizes common words.

TF-IDF handles both problems — it accounts for document-level frequency AND corpus-level rarity.

Why Generic Sentiment Fails

The Harvard General Inquirer (1960s) classifies words for everyday English. In financial text, this fails badly:

"liability"

Harvard: NEGATIVE

Reality: accounting term

"tax"

Harvard: NEGATIVE

Reality: line item

"capital"

Harvard: NEGATIVE

Reality: balance sheet

"foreign"

Harvard: NEGATIVE

Reality: geographic

73.8%

of Harvard "negative" words are NOT negative in financial text

Harvard GI vs. Loughran-McDonald

Class Poll

Which of these words is NEGATIVE in a 10-K annual filing?

A) "liability" — every company has liabilities

B) "litigation" — the company is being sued

C) "capital" — an accounting category

D) "foreign" — describes a geographic segment

Poll 2 Reveal

Answer: (B) "litigation" — being sued IS negative

The Harvard General Inquirer classifies all four as negative.

Only the Loughran-McDonald lexicon correctly identifies liability, capital, and foreign as neutral in financial context.

Lesson: Always use domain-specific lexicons for economic text.
