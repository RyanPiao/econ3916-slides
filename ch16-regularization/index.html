1  •  The Penalty Replaces the Search

You cannot try every model. So stop trying.

Why You Cannot Simply Try Every Model

Chapter 15's recipe: enumerate candidate models, cross-validate each, take the winner. Seven polynomial degrees is seven fits.

With \(p\) candidate variables the number of subsets is \(2^p\).

\(p = 20\)

about a million models. Tedious — but possible.

\(p = 500\)

\(2^{500}\) — a number with 151 digits.

Enumeration is impossible, and no faster computer changes that.

The Classical Answers, and Their Three Problems

Three older ways to choose variables without fitting all \(2^p\) models. On today’s data, \(n = 100\) rows and \(p = 500\) predictors:

Forward stepwise

Start with no variables. Each round, try adding each one left; keep the one that improves the fit most. Stop when none helps.

Round 1: try 500, keep 1 · Round 2: try 499, keep 1 · …

Runs. Ten rounds = 500 + 499 + … + 491 = 4,955 fits, not \(2^{{500}}\).

Backward elimination

Start with all variables. Each round, drop the least significant one (largest p-value). Stop when all are significant.

Round 1: fit all 500, drop 1 · Round 2: fit 499, drop 1 · …

Cannot start. All 500 on 100 rows is the perfect-fit regression: no p-values to drop by.

Best subset

Fit every subset and keep the best. The only one of the three that is exact.

\(p = 20\): about a million fits · \(p = 30\): about a billion

Stops at about \(p = 30\). At \(p = 500\) it is the \(2^{{500}}\) search itself.

So the count can be made small. But even forward stepwise, the one that runs, has three problems:

1. Unstable. Change a few rows and a different variable wins Round 1; every later round then differs.

2. Greedy. One addition at a time, so a variable useful only alongside another is never added.

3. Tests the data chose. Every round tests a hypothesis the data suggested: Chapter 8’s multiple comparisons.

Regularization fixes 1 and 2 — one convex problem, unique minimum, all coefficients determined at once. It does not fix 3. Hold that thought until Section 6.

Charge the Model for Complexity

Rather than searching over subsets, change the objective so the optimiser is charged for complexity — then solve one convex problem.

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2}_{\text{fit — as in Ch 12}} + \underbrace{\lambda \, P(\boldsymbol{\beta})}_{\text{fee for complexity}} \right\}$$

In words. Keep OLS's objective and add a fee proportional to how big the coefficients are. \(\lambda \geq 0\) is the price per unit of coefficient size; \(P(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) is how you measure "size" — and choosing \(P\) is choosing the method.

Choosing \(\lambda\) is choosing a point on Topic 15’s bias-variance curve, continuously.

Standardisation Is Mandatory, Not Stylistic

OLS is scale-invariant: measure income in dollars or in thousands of dollars and the fitted values are identical.

Penalised regression is not, because the penalty charges by coefficient magnitude, and a coefficient’s size depends on its variable’s units.

Always standardise — and always inside a pipeline, so the test set never touches the scaler, for exactly Chapter 15’s leakage reasons. To refit it inside every CV fold too, put the pipeline inside the CV, as Lab 16’s Step 6 does with GridSearchCV.

model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LassoCV(cv=5))

Poll: The Scaler You Forgot

You fit LASSO on a wage regression. experience is in years (0–40). income_last_year is in dollars (0–200,000). You skip StandardScaler.

What happens?

A) Nothing — LASSO is scale-invariant like OLS
B) income_last_year is much more likely to be zeroed
C) experience is much more likely to be zeroed
D) Both get zeroed; the intercept absorbs everything

Answer: C — experience Is the One Dropped

Because income_last_year is measured in large units, its coefficient is tiny — pennies of wage per dollar of income — so the penalty barely charges for it and it stays in. experience, in small units, carries a large coefficient, is charged heavily, and is shrunk away.

Why B is tempting but wrong: "big variable, big penalty" reverses the mechanism. The penalty charges the coefficient, not the variable — and a variable in large units has a small coefficient. Get this backwards and you will misread every unstandardised LASSO you ever see.

The same variable in dollars has a coefficient a thousand times smaller than in thousands, so it is charged a thousandth as much: without standardisation the penalty favours whatever is measured in large units, with no warning.

2  •  Ridge: Shrink Everything

One term added to a matrix, and \(p > n\) stops being fatal.

Ridge — the \(L_2\) Penalty

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{ridge}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ \underbrace{\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2}_{\text{fit}} + \underbrace{\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2}_{\text{fee on squared size}} \right\}$$

The data. Four points: \(x\) = 1, 2, 3, 4 and \(y\) = 2, 3, 5, 6. One slope \(b\); the intercept is free, never charged.

Step 1 · The cost of a slope \(b\). Fit \(= 10 - 14b + 5b^2\), plus fee \(\lambda b^2\). Squared: twice the slope pays four times the fee.

Step 2 · \(\lambda = 0\). No fee, so \(b = 1.4\): the OLS slope, fit 0.2.

Step 3 · \(\lambda = 5\). Keep 1.4: 0.2 + 9.8 = 10.0. Shrink to 0.7: 2.65 + 2.45 = 5.1. Shrinking is cheaper.

Step 4 · The rule. \(b = 7/(5+\lambda)\). The bigger \(\lambda\), the smaller the slope.

One Term, and an Impossible Problem Becomes Solvable

OLS

$$(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}$$

RIDGE

$$(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}$$

OLS needs the inverse of \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\). It fails when a column repeats another, and whenever \(p > n\). Our four points, with \(x\) put in twice:

The data: \(x\) entered twice

\(\mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}-1.5&-1.5\\-0.5&-0.5\\0.5&0.5\\1.5&1.5\end{bmatrix}\;\mathbf{y}=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\-1\\1\\2\end{bmatrix}\)

the four points, centred, so no intercept column

Step 1 · \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\)

\(\begin{bmatrix}5&5\\5&5\end{bmatrix}\)

determinant 5·5 − 5·5 = 0: no inverse

Step 2 · add \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\), \(\lambda = 5\)

\(\begin{bmatrix}10&5\\5&10\end{bmatrix}\)

determinant 100 − 25 = 75

Step 3 · \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}\)

\(\begin{bmatrix}7\\7\end{bmatrix}\)

the same 7 as on the Ridge slide

Step 4 · invert, multiply

\(\tfrac{1}{75}\begin{bmatrix}10&-5\\-5&10\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}7\\7\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.467\\0.467\end{bmatrix}\)

the twins split the slope evenly

\(\lambda\) on the diagonal lifts every eigenvalue by \(\lambda\): 10 and 0 become 15 and 5. None can be zero, so the inverse exists for any \(\lambda > 0\). With one column, \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{X}\) is just 5 and \(\mathbf{X}'\mathbf{y}\) is 7: the \(7/(5+\lambda)\) rule.

Ridge does more than help with multicollinearity: it removes the condition that made it fatal.

The Geometry: a Budget You Cannot Exceed

The same problem, written as a budget: the best fit, subject to a limit on coefficient size.

$$\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\ \|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 \le s$$

1

Each ellipse is a set of coefficients with the same fit. The centre is OLS, the best fit.

2

The circle is the budget, \(\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 \le s\). A bigger \(\lambda\) means a smaller circle.

3

Ridge is where the smallest ellipse first touches the circle: the best fit the budget allows.

Why Shrinking Toward a Wrong Value Helps

True coefficient 2.0. Your estimator is unbiased with s.e. 3.0. Unbiased means right on average — and that a typical draw returns 5.1, or −0.4, or 4.3.

Now shrink every estimate by half. It is now biased — it averages 1.0 against a truth of 2.0 — but its s.e. is 1.5.

Bias² rose a little, 0 → 1.00. Variance fell a lot, 9.00 → 2.25. Their sum fell, 9.00 → 3.25.

Shrinkage is worthwhile exactly when your estimates are noisy — which in high dimensions they always are.

Where Ridge Shines, and Where It Cannot Help

Right tool when many predictors each contribute a little and they are correlated — it shrinks correlated groups together rather than picking arbitrarily between them.

Wrong tool when most predictors are irrelevant — and the reason is structural, not about tuning.

Uniform shrinkage cannot create sparsity. No \(\lambda\) makes 490 useless coefficients disappear while leaving 10 useful ones intact.

43.99

OLS test MSE

43.28

Ridge test MSE, best \(\lambda\) over eight orders of magnitude

A 2% improvement on a catastrophe.

A Hyperparameter at the Edge of Its Grid Is Information

Offered penalties from \(10^{-2}\) to \(10^{6}\), cross-validation selected the smallest one — \(\lambda = 0.01\), the floor of the grid.

That is not a bug. Faced with a problem Ridge cannot solve, CV correctly reported that more shrinkage does not help: every large \(\lambda\) was worse, so it chose the least-bad option available.

Rule of thumb. When cross-validation selects a value at the edge of your grid, either the grid is too narrow or the method is wrong for the problem. Widening it to \(10^{8}\) changed nothing here — which rules out the first and leaves the second.

A selected hyperparameter sitting at a boundary is information, not a result to report and move past.

Poll: Counting Zeros

You fit Ridge with a large \(\lambda\) on 500 standardised predictors, 490 of which are pure noise.

How many coefficients come back exactly zero?

A) About 490 — that is what the penalty is for
B) Exactly 10 — the true ones survive
C) Zero, or essentially zero, no matter how large \(\lambda\) gets
D) It depends on \(\lambda\) — large enough \(\lambda\) zeroes them

Answer: C — None of Them

The constraint region is a circle. It has no corners, so the point where the expanding loss contours first touch it is almost never on an axis — and a coefficient is exactly zero only on an axis. All 490 noise variables stay in the model at reduced weight: Ridge shrinks every coefficient toward zero and sets none exactly to zero.

Why D is tempting but wrong: \(\lambda\to\infty\) does drive every coefficient toward zero — including the ten real ones. There is no \(\lambda\) that zeroes the 490 while sparing the 10. That is structural, not a tuning problem.

3  •  LASSO: Shrink and Select

One exponent changed. The consequence is qualitative.

LASSO — the \(L_1\) Penalty

Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (Tibshirani, 1996)

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\text{lasso}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ \tfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} |\beta_j| \right\}$$

One exponent changed, from \(\beta_j^2\) to \(|\beta_j|\).

The same four points. Ridge’s slope \(7/(5+\lambda)\) only shrinks. LASSO’s slope is \(1.4 - 0.8\lambda\), and from \(\lambda = 1.75\) on it is exactly 0.

The consequence is qualitative rather than quantitative: a shorter list, not just smaller numbers.

The Same Picture, One Shape Changed

Expanding elliptical contours meeting a diamond are very likely to touch at a corner, because corners stick out. And a corner of the diamond is a point where one coordinate is exactly zero.

Why the False Positives Appear — and Why They Must

Among 490 irrelevant predictors, each independent of the outcome, some will correlate with \(y\) in this sample by chance alone. With 490 draws, a few will correlate quite strongly. LASSO cannot distinguish "correlates because it causes" from "correlates because we got unlucky" — it sees only the sample.

To exclude every lucky noise variable, the penalty would have to exclude anything with a modest sample correlation. But some relevant variables also have modest sample correlations — particularly those with smaller true coefficients. A penalty aggressive enough to remove all the noise removes real signal along with it.

So the real choice is between a short list that misses true variables and a longer one that admits false ones.

And note the design: those 34 false positives occur on independent Gaussian columns — the friendliest possible case. Real economic data, where everything correlates with everything, is worse.

Common Misconception

"LASSO tells me which variables matter."

It tells you which variables it kept, which is a different thing. Here it kept 44 to find 10.

And the selection carries a formal caveat: LASSO is only guaranteed to recover the true set of variables when the irrelevant ones are not strongly correlated with the relevant ones — a condition routinely violated by macroeconomic series, asset-pricing factors, and anything with a factor structure.

Treat a LASSO selection as a hypothesis worth investigating — never as a finding.

Discussion: Is Your Question Sparse or Dense?

A penalty is not a neutral technical device. It encodes a belief about the world.

\(L_1\) believes most coefficients are exactly zero. \(L_2\) believes many are small but nonzero. Neither is a safe default.

4  •  Choosing \(\lambda\)

Where the right variables and the right predictions part company.

\(\lambda\) Is a Hyperparameter

Why not choose \(\lambda\) by minimising the objective? That always returns \(\lambda = 0\): with no fee, the fit to the training data is always best.

So \(\lambda\) is a hyperparameter, chosen the way Topic 15 chose model complexity: by cross-validation.

The fits along the grid are the regularization path. LassoCV and RidgeCV do all four steps in one call.

Two Objectives, Two Different \(\lambda\)

If you want the right variables

\(\lambda = 1.0\)

10 kept, 10 true, 0 false — and test MSE 6.92

If you want the best predictions

\(\lambda = 0.138\)

44 kept, 34 of them noise — and test MSE 1.54

Selecting the right variables and predicting well are different objectives, optimised at different \(\lambda\) — and here they are in direct conflict. Cross-validation targets prediction, because that is what you asked it for.

If you want the variable list, you are asking a question this procedure was not tuned to answer.

Poll: The Paradox in the Table

At \(\lambda = 1.0\), LASSO recovers exactly the 10 true variables and nothing else — yet its test MSE (6.92) is 4.5× worse than \(\lambda = 0.138\), which keeps 44.

Why?

A) The test set is too small to rank them reliably
B) At \(\lambda = 1.0\) the ten survivors are heavily shrunk, so the magnitudes are badly attenuated
C) The 34 extra variables carry real signal the 10 miss
D) Cross-validation picked the wrong \(\lambda\)

Answer: B — Right Variables, Wrong Magnitudes

A large penalty does two things at once — it zeroes some coefficients and it shrinks the survivors. At \(\lambda = 1.0\) the right variables are in the model with magnitudes far too small, so predictions come out systematically flat.

Why D is tempting but wrong: CV chose \(\lambda = 0.138\), and the test set — which CV never saw — puts its lowest of these rows at \(\lambda = 0.138\). CV did its job perfectly. It optimised prediction, because that is what you asked for. Nothing is broken.

5  •  The Penalty Is a Belief

Change nothing but the truth, and the ranking reverses.

Same Predictors, Only the Truth Changed

Dense truth: all 500 features matter, each a little. LASSO is now the worst of the three — a third worse than OLS, on data where OLS is already poor.

A Penalty Encodes a Belief

The \(L_1\) penalty believes

most coefficients are exactly zero.

When true, enormously powerful. When false, it is forcing zeros onto parameters that are not zero — paying in bias for nothing.

The \(L_2\) penalty believes

many coefficients are small but nonzero.

Right whenever effects are diffuse — which describes a great deal of economics, where outcomes are shaped by many weakly-acting factors.

So the choice turns on your subject matter, not on statistics: do I believe a small number of these variables do essentially all the work? Sometimes yes — a few genes, a few binding constraints. Often no.

Correlated Predictors: What Actually Goes Wrong

The standard claim is that Ridge handles correlated predictors better than LASSO. The truth is more specific — and more useful.

Elastic Net — Let the Data Choose the Mix

$$\lambda \left( \alpha_{EN} \sum_j |\beta_j| + \tfrac{1 - \alpha_{EN}}{2}\sum_j \beta_j^2 \right)$$

In words. Pay a blend of the two fees. \(\alpha_{EN} = 1\) is pure LASSO, \(\alpha_{EN} = 0\) is pure Ridge. The \(L_1\) part preserves sparsity; the \(L_2\) part supplies a grouping effect, pulling correlated predictors toward each other rather than letting one win arbitrarily.

On the correlated data above it kept all 8 (mix chosen: 0.7) and spread them more evenly — the blue bars on the previous slide.

enet = ElasticNetCV(cv=5, l1_ratio=[0.1, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95, 1.0]).fit(X, y)
print("mixing parameter chosen on the sparse design:", enet.l1_ratio_)
mixing parameter chosen on the sparse design: 1.0

The chosen mixing parameter is itself a result worth reporting. Near 1 says the data looks sparse; near 0, that effects are diffuse.

Economist at Work — the IMF, 2025

Post-pandemic structural breaks had made the reliable data short while the number of available high-frequency indicators had grown past a hundred. With \(p\) approaching \(n\), the design matrix was rank-deficient: OLS was not merely inaccurate but incomputable.

The team deployed LASSO, Ridge and Elastic Net. The first result was disappointing — on highly collinear macro series, LASSO selected variables that made no economic sense.

What fixed it was not a better algorithm. The economists imposed sign restrictions — consumer sentiment must enter positively, unemployment claims negatively, and so on wherever theory dictates a direction. With those constraints the model outperformed both MIDAS and dynamic-factor benchmarks.

An algorithm turned loose on collinear data finds patterns. An economist decides which patterns are permitted.

6  •  What You May Not Do Afterwards

For economists, the most consequential thing in the chapter.

A Workflow That Sounds Completely Reasonable

You have 500 candidate controls. You run LASSO; it keeps 12. You fit ordinary OLS on those 12, report the coefficients with their standard errors and \(p\)-values, and describe the result as a regression with well-chosen controls.

This is invalid, and the invalidity is not subtle.

Take thirty seconds. What is wrong with it?

You Used the Data Twice

Classical inference assumes the specification was fixed before seeing the data. Chapter 8 built every \(p\)-value on that assumption.

But those 12 variables were chosen because they correlated strongly with the outcome in this sample — that is what LASSO does. Reporting classical standard errors treats a data-driven choice as if theory had made it, and the intervals come out too narrow. Their true coverage is well below nominal.

It is the same error as running 500 hypothesis tests and reporting only the significant ones — which Chapter 8 named. LASSO simply automates the selection and makes it feel principled.

Worse than the manual version in one respect: a researcher running 500 tests by hand knows they did it. LASSO performs the same search without telling you and returns a tidy list — there is no artefact to audit.

What to Do Instead Depends on What You Want

For prediction

Nothing is wrong. Report cross-validated error and make no claims about individual coefficients.

For a treatment effect

You need post-double selection: run LASSO twice — once predicting the outcome from the controls, once predicting the treatment — and use the union of both sets in an unpenalised regression.

Belloni, Chernozhukov & Hansen (2014). The machinery is ECON 5200's and Chapter 24's — recognising that you need it is today's.

And it gains less than you would expect. A 2024 World Bank audit re-estimated 780 treatment effects from published randomised trials using post-double selection. Against plain ANCOVA with pre-specified controls it improved standard errors by under 1% — and in over half the cases the treatment equation selected no controls at all.

That second finding has a simple explanation: in a randomised trial assignment is random by construction, so nothing should predict it. The second LASSO has nothing to find.

What Regularization Does Not Fix

1. A confounded design. Shrinkage reduces variance. It says nothing about bias from an omitted confounder — and it makes it harder to see, because a coefficient shrunk toward zero looks modest and well-behaved whether it is correctly estimated or badly confounded.

2. Causal identification. LASSO selects variables that predict. A variable can predict because it causes the outcome, because it shares a cause, or because it is a consequence of the outcome — Chapter 10's three structures, indistinguishable to any penalty. Hand it a collider and it will keep it.

3. A badly measured target. Chapter 15's noise floor is unmoved by any penalty. If \(y\) is measured with substantial error, the way past that limit is better data, not a better estimator.

And one for reading papers. Regularized coefficients are not comparable across specifications the way OLS coefficients are. Change \(\lambda\), the candidate set, or the scaling, and every coefficient moves — each is the solution to a different constrained problem. Reporting a LASSO coefficient without its \(\lambda\), its standardisation and its candidate set is reporting an uninterpretable number.

Poll: What May You Report?

Which of these is a legitimate thing to report after a cross-validated LASSO?

A) "These 44 variables are the drivers of the outcome"
B) "LASSO retained 44 of 500 predictors at \(\lambda = 0.138\); test MSE on held-out data was 1.54"

7  •  The Workflow, and Your Lab

Six sections of theory. Here is what to actually do.

A Regularization Workflow

1

Decide what you want before you fit. Prediction, or a coefficient you intend to interpret? Most confusion comes from wanting both and admitting to neither.

2

Standardise, inside a pipeline. One line handles the scale-dependence and the leakage together.

3

Fit Ridge and LASSO both, and let cross-validation compare them. The comparison is itself informative about sparsity.

4

Look at the CV curve, not just its minimum. Flat across an order of magnitude means \(\lambda\) is not identified — say so.

5

Report the number selected, and treat the list as a hypothesis. "Retained 44 of 500" is a finding. "Identified the 44 that matter" is not.

6

Three Takeaways

1

The penalty replaces the search. \(2^{500}\) subsets is impossible; one convex problem with a fee on coefficient size is not. Ridge's \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) even removes the invertibility requirement that made \(p > n\) fatal.

2

The penalty is an assumption, not an upgrade. \(L_1\) asserts the truth is sparse, \(L_2\) that it is dense. On a sparse truth LASSO beat OLS 28×; change only the truth and it came last of three.

3

Selection is not the same objective as prediction. The \(\lambda\) that found exactly the right 10 predicted 4.5× worse than the \(\lambda\) that kept 44. Report the list as a hypothesis, and never attach classical \(p\)-values to it.
