B1
Data, measurement and robust statistics · Topics 1–4

Structure beats size

Cross-sections compare, time series track change, panels do both. A million rows from one moment are still a cross-section. Tidy data: each variable a column, each observation a row.

Indexes compress

Laspeyres freezes the basket, with a known upward substitution bias. Put both numbers in the same year's dollars before you divide. Goodhart: a measure that becomes a target stops measuring.

Plot first

Mean, SD and correlation describe centre, spread and linear association only; the Datasaurus Dozen shares all five to two decimals. Lie Factor = effect shown ÷ effect in the data.

Robust statistics

The mean breaks down at \(1/n\): one value can move it anywhere. Median and MAD survive up to half the file. Tukey fences: \(Q_1 - 1.5\times\mathrm{IQR}\) and \(Q_3 + 1.5\times\mathrm{IQR}\); a flag says unusual, not wrong.

Data, measurement and robust statistics · Practice #1 · Multiple choice

Q. A spreadsheet tracks the quarterly unemployment rate for Massachusetts alone, from the first quarter of 2005 through the last quarter of 2025. What type of data is this?

A) A cross-section

A cross-section observes many units at one moment, such as all 50 states in one quarter. Here there is one unit.

B) A panel

A panel follows the same many units over time. One state is one unit, so there is nothing to compare across units.

C) A time series

D) A randomized experiment

Random assignment is about how the data arose, not its structure. Nothing was assigned here.

Step 1 · Method

Ask the two questions behind Topic 1's three structures: how many units, and how many periods? Many units at one moment is a cross-section; one unit over many periods is a time series; the same units over many periods is a panel.

Step 2 · Work

One unit (Massachusetts) and 21 years × 4 quarters = 84 periods: a time series. Add the other 49 states for the same quarters and the file becomes a panel. Survey 50 states in one quarter and it is a cross-section.

Answer

C   A time series: one unit observed across 84 quarters.

Data, measurement and robust statistics · Practice #3 · Multiple choice

Q. A call center sets a target on average handle time per call. Within a month the average falls, while repeat calls and complaints rise because agents rush callers off the line. This is an example of:

A) Hedonic adjustment bias

Hedonic adjustment is how the BLS prices a product whose quality changes. No price index is involved here.

B) A selection effect

A selection effect is about who ends up in the data. The same calls are measured; what changed is how agents behave once the number is rewarded.

C) Axis truncation

Truncation is a charting choice (Topic 3). The handle time itself fell; no chart is involved.

D) Goodhart's Law at work

Step 1 · Method

Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The deck names three mechanisms, each with its own fix: optimisation against the measure, gaming, and obsolescence.

Step 2 · Work

Agents lowered handle time without serving callers better: mechanism 2, gaming. The fix for gaming is a counter-metric the same behaviour cannot move in the same direction, such as repeat calls per caller, which rose while handle time fell. That divergence is what the Wells Fargo poll's customer-initiated share would have shown.

Answer

D   D: Goodhart's Law, by gaming; a repeat-call counter-metric would have caught it.

Q. A one-bedroom apartment rented for USD 900 a month in 2008 and USD 1,600 a month in 2024. The CPI (annual average, base 1982–84 = 100) was 215.3 in 2008 and 313.7 in 2024. By what percent did the real rent change from 2008 to 2024? Enter a percent with one decimal, without the % sign.

Your answer:  

Step 1 · Method

Put both rents in the same year's dollars: divide by the CPI of the year the rent was paid, multiply by the CPI of the year you want. Then compare. Both CPIs must be on the same base; the base cancels.

Step 2 · Work

900 ÷ 215.3 × 313.7 = 1,311.3 in 2024 dollars. Real change = 1,600 ÷ 1,311.3 − 1 = 0.220, so 22.0%. The nominal rise is 77.8%. Subtracting inflation (77.8% − 45.7% = 32.1%) is only a rough shortcut and overstates the real rise here: price changes compound, so divide, do not subtract.

Answer

22.0 (accepted within ±0.15)   About 22.0%: the 2008 rent is worth USD 1,311 in 2024 dollars, and 1,600 is 22.0% above that.

Data, measurement and robust statistics · Practice #7 · Matching

Q. Match each Topic 4 tool to what it computes or does. One option on the right is not used.

1. Interquartile range

2. Scaled MAD

3. Robust z-score

4. Isolation Forest

A) The width of the middle half of the data, \(Q_3 - Q_1\)

B) An observation's distance from the median, divided by 1.4826 times the MAD

C) The square root of the average squared distance from the mean, with a breakdown point of \(1/n\)

D) Flags an observation that a few random splits isolate, even when each of its values is ordinary on its own

E) The median absolute deviation times 1.4826, a robust stand-in for the standard deviation

Step 1 · Method

Each robust tool swaps a fragile part for one that a few extreme rows cannot move: the middle half instead of the whole range, the median instead of the mean, many random questions instead of one variable at a time.

Step 2 · Work

IQR = \(Q_3 - Q_1\). MAD is the median distance from the median; times 1.4826 (that is 1/0.6745) it estimates \(\sigma\) on normal data. The robust z-score puts the median and scaled MAD where \(\bar x\) and \(s\) were, so outliers cannot inflate the yardstick used to find them. Isolation Forest catches the 2-bedroom, 10-room home that a fence on either variable alone misses. The left-over option is the standard deviation.

Answer

1→A; 2→E; 3→B; 4→D   IQR → A, scaled MAD → E, robust z-score → B, Isolation Forest → D; C (the standard deviation) is not used.

Data, measurement and robust statistics · Practice #8 · Numerical answer

Q. Commute times (minutes) for 40 students: df['commute'].describe() prints count 40, mean 31.6, std 22.8, min 6, 25% 18, 50% 27, 75% 38, max 140. Using Tukey's rule with the usual 1.5, what is the lower fence, in minutes? Enter a number.

Your answer:  

Step 1 · Method

Tukey fences: lower = \(Q_1 - 1.5\times\mathrm{IQR}\), upper = \(Q_3 + 1.5\times\mathrm{IQR}\), with \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\) the 25% and 75% rows of describe(). The 1.5 is convention, not law.

Step 2 · Work

IQR = 38 − 18 = 20. Lower fence = 18 − 1.5 × 20 = −12. A commute cannot be negative, so nothing is flagged at the short end. The upper fence, 38 + 30 = 68, is the one that matters here: the maximum, 140, is far beyond it, and the mean of 31.6 sits above the median of 27 because the long commutes pull it up. A fence flags a value as unusual, not wrong: check the 140 before deciding what to do with it, and write the choice down.

Answer

-12 (accepted within ±0.01)   −12: 18 − 1.5 × 20. Nothing can sit below it, since commutes are never negative; the upper fence, 68, flags the 140.

Probability and biased samples · Topics 5–6

Bayes needs the base rate

A test that catches 99% of fraud and flags 2% of legitimate transactions is right 4.7% of the time it fires when 0.1% are fraud. Rarity beats accuracy.

Simulated numbers have a standard error

\(\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) for a proportion from \(n\) draws, so error falls as \(1/\sqrt{n}\). Do not rank two simulated numbers within about two SEs.

Design, not size

Meng's identity: error = how bent the selection × how spread out the outcome × how much you missed. More data shrinks only the last term.

One condition, four costumes

Selection, survivorship, nonresponse and self-selection are all \(\mathrm{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0\). Ask: who is missing, and is their absence related to the answer?

Probability and biased samples · Practice #1 · Multiple choice

Q. A screening model flags 95% of loans that will later default and wrongly flags 5% of loans that will be repaid. Only 1% of loans in the portfolio default. If a loan is flagged, roughly what is the probability that it will actually default?

A) 95%

Reads the hit rate, P(flag | default), as P(default | flag). That swap is the base-rate fallacy.

B) Roughly one in two

No reason for even odds: the false alarms come from the repaid loans, a pool 99 times larger than the defaults.

C) About 1%

That is the base rate before the flag. A flag raises the chance from 1% to about 16%.

D) About 16%

Step 1 · Method

The deck's three lines: the law of total probability gives P(flag); Bayes gives the real flags as a share of all flags.

Step 2 · Work

Real flags: 0.95 × 0.01 = 0.0095. False flags: 0.05 × 0.99 = 0.0495. P(flag) = 0.0590. P(default | flag) = 0.0095 ÷ 0.0590 = 0.161. Per 10,000 loans: 100 default and 95 of them are flagged; 9,900 are repaid and 495 are flagged; 95 of 590 flags are real. Rarity beats accuracy, as in the deck's fraud example (4.7%).

Answer

D   D: about 16%, because false alarms from the large pool of repaid loans outnumber the real defaults about five to one.

Probability and biased samples · Practice #2 · Multiple choice

Q. To check a 95% confidence-interval procedure, an analyst simulates 1,000 samples from a population whose mean she set herself. 938 of the 1,000 intervals contain that mean: 93.8%. What does this run show about the procedure?

A) The procedure is too narrow, because 938 is below 950

938 below 950 is what a correct procedure produces in many runs. Coverage from 1,000 simulated samples is itself an estimate: about 95% of runs of a correct procedure fall between 936 and 964.

B) Coverage of 93.8% proves the intervals are 1.2 points too narrow, so they should be widened by that much

The 1.2-point gap is 1.7 Monte Carlo standard errors, the size of gap noise produces all the time. Widening by it would chase noise.

C) The 1,000-sample coverage figure has its own standard error of about 0.7 points, so 93.8% is within two of them of 95% and this run cannot tell the procedure is off

D) Rerun the 1,000 samples with new seeds until coverage comes out at 95%, then report that run

Picking the run that comes out right is reporting the seed. Report this run with its standard error, or run more samples.

Step 1 · Method

A simulated proportion has a standard error, \(\mathrm{SE} = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). Do not call two numbers different when they sit within about two SEs of each other.

Step 2 · Work

\(\sqrt{0.95 \times 0.05 / 1{,}000} = 0.0069\), about 0.7 points. Gap: 95.0% − 93.8% = 1.2 points = 1.7 SE. Inside two SEs, so this run cannot separate a correct procedure from a slightly short one. To tell, run many more samples: 10,000 cuts the SE to about 0.2 points.

Answer

C   C: the gap is about 1.7 Monte Carlo standard errors, so 1,000 samples cannot show the procedure is off.

Probability and biased samples · Practice #3 · True / false

Q. True or false: a properly random sample of 500 voters can estimate a population mean more accurately than a self-selected online poll of 3 million respondents, because a larger sample does not remove selection bias.

True

False

This is “more data always helps”. A bigger sample shrinks only the third term; with a bent selection the big poll settles on the wrong number, with a narrow interval around it.

Step 1 · Method

Write the error as the deck's product of three, bent × spread × missed: \(\rho_{R,Y} \times \sigma_Y \times \sqrt{(N-n)/n}\). Ask which term each design changes.

Step 2 · Work

The random 500 has \(\rho_{R,Y} = 0\) by design, so its error is sampling noise that its margin of error describes. The online poll's respondents chose themselves, one of the four costumes of \(\mathrm{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0\); 3 million responses shrink \(\sqrt{(N-n)/n}\) and leave \(\rho_{R,Y}\,\sigma_Y\) in place. The deck's hook: 2.3 million responses worth about 400 random ones, from a correlation of −0.005.

Answer

True   True: design, not size, decides whether the estimate can be trusted.

Probability and biased samples · Practice #4 · Multiple choice

Q. Nonresponse bias occurs when:

A) Respondents give deliberately inaccurate answers

Wrong answers from people in the sample are measurement error: the person is there, the recorded value is wrong.

B) Units that exited the system before data collection are never observed

Units that left before you collected data: survivorship bias, the deck's costume 1.

C) Those who decline to take part differ systematically from those who take part

D) The sampling frame leaves out part of the population

A frame that misses people is selection at the frame stage: the gap between target population and sampling frame, which more data does not close.

Step 1 · Method

Selection, survivorship, nonresponse and self-selection are one condition, \(\mathrm{Cov}(R_i, Y_i) \neq 0\). Tell them apart by when and how a unit went missing.

Step 2 · Work

Nonresponse: the unit was in the frame and was asked, then declined, and decliners differ in the outcome (high earners skipping an income survey pull the mean down). With response rates of 1–5% in 2024 (Pew), the question is how big the bias is and in which direction, not whether it exists.

Answer

C   C: the people who decline differ systematically from those who answer.

Estimates, intervals and tests · Topics 7–8

SD describes people; SE your estimate

\(\mathrm{SE}(\bar x) = s/\sqrt{n}\): only the SE shrinks with \(n\). The 95% belongs to the procedure, not to the one interval you built.

The bootstrap resamples

\(n\) draws with replacement, many times; each resample holds about 63.2% of the rows. Use it for statistics with no formula, such as the median. It measures variance, never bias.

p is P(data | H0)

A small p says the data would be surprising if \(H_0\) were true; it is not the chance \(H_0\) is true. Use Welch's t by default and report Cohen's d beside p.

Power comes first

Power analysis happens before data collection. An underpowered null is not a finding, and twenty tests at 0.05 manufacture findings.

Estimates, intervals and tests · Practice #1 · Multiple choice

Q. An analyst bootstraps the median of a sample of 250 household incomes. Each bootstrap resample should consist of:

A) 250 draws taken with replacement from the sample, so some incomes repeat and others are left out

B) 250 draws taken without replacement, which only reorders the original sample

Without replacement, 250 draws from 250 incomes return the same sample in a new order: the median never changes.

C) A fresh set of 250 incomes collected from the population

Fresh data from the population is what you cannot afford. The bootstrap uses the sample in place of the population.

D) The incomes that lie inside the interquartile range of the sample

Dropping the tails changes the data. The bootstrap resamples all 250 observations.

Step 1 · Method

Efron (1979): treat the sample as if it were the population and resample it, \(n\) draws with replacement, many times; compute the statistic on each resample.

Step 2 · Work

Here \(n = 250\). The share of distinct rows in one resample is about \(1 - (1 - 1/250)^{250} = 0.633\), so about 37% are left out each time. The SD of the medians across, say, 9,999 resamples is the SE of the median, a statistic with no simple formula. The bootstrap measures variance, never bias: a biased sample gives a tight interval around the wrong number.

Answer

A   A: 250 draws with replacement from the sample.

Q. True or false: if the same sampling and interval procedure were repeated many times, about 90% of the 90% confidence intervals produced would contain the true population mean.

True

False

This denies the correct reading. The reading that fails is a different one: that one realised interval has a 90% chance of holding the mean.

Step 1 · Method

The truth is fixed; the interval is the random thing. The confidence level describes how the interval-building recipe behaves over many runs.

Step 2 · Work

Repeat the procedure 1,000 times and about 900 intervals cover the mean. The count itself varies: its SE is \(\sqrt{0.9 \times 0.1 / 1{,}000} = 0.009\), so anything from about 881 to 919 is within two SEs. For any one interval you built, the answer is already yes or no.

Answer

Estimates, intervals and tests · Practice #3 · Multiple choice

Q. A job-training pilot raises average weekly earnings by USD 40 (two-sided p = 0.21; 95% CI −22.5 to 102.5). Which reading is right?

A) The training has no effect on earnings

Failing to reject the null leaves no effect as one possibility among many: the interval also holds gains of up to about USD 100.

B) There is a 79% chance the training works

One minus p is not the chance the effect is real; turning the p-value around needs Bayes and a prior.

C) The data fit no effect and also gains of up to about USD 100 a week; this study cannot tell them apart

D) The pilot shows the training works, because the estimate is positive

A positive estimate with p = 0.21 sits inside the noise: the interval runs below zero.

Step 1 · Method

A p-value above \(\alpha\) means fail to reject \(H_0\), which is not evidence that \(H_0\) is true. The confidence interval shows every effect the data are consistent with.

Step 2 · Work

p = 0.21 > 0.05: fail to reject. The 95% interval, −22.5 to 102.5, contains zero and also gains near USD 100 a week. Both fit the data, so the pilot was too small to tell them apart. Report the estimate with its interval, and say the study is underpowered for effects below about USD 100.

Answer

C   C: the interval runs from a small loss to a gain of about USD 100, so the pilot cannot separate no effect from a large one.

Estimates, intervals and tests · Practice #4 · Multiple choice

Q. Two groups have sample variances that differ by a factor of eight and unequal sizes. An analyst runs scipy.stats.ttest_ind with the default equal_var=True. What is the consequence?

A) The test pools the two variances into one estimate, so its p-value can mislead; equal_var=False, Welch's version, would keep the variances separate and adjust the degrees of freedom

B) Nothing changes, because the t-test is exact whatever the variances are

Student's t assumes equal variances; with unequal ones it misleads, which is why the deck says to use Welch's by default.

C) The function detects the imbalance and switches to a paired test

Nothing switches. A paired test is for two measurements on the same units, a design question, not a variance one.

D) SciPy already lowers the degrees of freedom for unequal variances, so the p-value is conservative

SciPy lowers the degrees of freedom only when you ask for Welch's. With the default, nothing is adjusted.

Step 1 · Method

Welch's \(t = (\bar x_1 - \bar x_2) / \sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}\): each group keeps its own variance. Student's pools them. With equal variances the two agree; with unequal ones Student's misleads.

Step 2 · Work

On Lab 8's LaLonde data (SDs 7,867 and 5,484) pooling gave an SE of 633 and p = 0.0048; Welch gave 671 and p = 0.0079. A factor of eight in the variances is a bigger gap than that. Welch's repairs unequal variance and nothing else; it does not fix dependence between observations.

Answer

A   A: the default pools the variances; set equal_var=False for Welch's.

Experiments, DAGs and what to control for · Topics 9–10

Randomisation removes selection bias

Naive gap = ATE + selection bias. A coin flip makes the second term zero; adding controls does not. LaLonde: +1,794 randomised, about −15,000 against the general population.

Power, and four failures

Halve the effect you want to detect and you need four times the users. Peeking, Goodhart, sample ratio mismatch and interference are not fixed by more data. Significance is not a decision.

Three explanations, no test

Causation, confounding and selection can produce the same correlation. Backdoor criterion: block every backdoor path, and control for nothing downstream of the treatment.

Conditioning opens colliders

Controlling for, stratifying on or filtering to a common effect creates an association. Ask: causes the treatment? causes the outcome? happened before it? “Improves fit” is not on the list.

Experiments, DAGs and what to control for · Practice #1 · Multiple choice

Q. An experiment is designed as a 90/10 split, with 10% of users held out as a control. Of 400,000 users, 42,800 land in control instead of the expected 40,000, and a chi-squared test of the split gives p < 0.0001. What is the right next step?

A) Keep the result, since a larger control group only adds power

The extra users are the symptom. A split this far from plan means assignment or logging is sorting users, and whatever sorts them is probably related to the outcome.

B) Apply post-stratification weights to restore the 90/10 ratio, then read the effect

Weights fix the ratio, not the reason users landed in the wrong arm. No adjustment repairs a broken randomisation.

C) Halt the experiment and find the pipeline or logging bug before reading any outcome metric

D) Extend the test by a week so that the ratio can drift back toward the plan

Waiting adds users through the same broken pipeline; the mismatch does not drift back by itself.

Step 1 · Method

Compare the observed split with the designed one using a chi-squared test. On hundreds of thousands of users, even a small percentage gap is far too big to be chance.

Step 2 · Work

Expected 40,000 control and 360,000 treatment; observed 42,800 and 357,200. \(\chi^2 = 2{,}800^2/40{,}000 + 2{,}800^2/360{,}000 = 196 + 21.8 = 217.8\), p far below 0.0001. A control share of 10.7% against 10.0% is a bug. Every number on that scorecard is suspect, including the ones that look fine.

Answer

C   C: halt and find the bug before reading any outcome.

Experiments, DAGs and what to control for · Practice #2 · Numerical answer

Q. A city offers a free money-management course. Residents who sign up save USD 1,900 a year more than residents who do not. A later randomised trial of the same course, on the same kind of residents, finds that it raises saving by USD 400 a year. Assume the course has the same effect on everyone. Using naive gap = ATE + selection bias, how large is the selection-bias term, in dollars a year? Enter a number (with a minus sign if it is negative).

Your answer:  

Step 1 · Method

Topic 9's decomposition: \(E[Y \mid D{=}1] - E[Y \mid D{=}0] = \text{ATE} + \big(E[Y(0) \mid D{=}1] - E[Y(0) \mid D{=}0]\big)\). The randomised trial measures the ATE, because random assignment makes the second term zero.

Step 2 · Work

1,900 = 400 + selection bias, so selection bias = 1,900 − 400 = +1,500. The people who chose the course would have saved 1,500 a year more even without it. Selection bias has no default sign: in the deck's LaLonde case it ran the other way, turning +1,794 into about −15,000.

Answer

1500 (accepted within ±1)   1,500: the course's own effect is 400 a year; the other 1,500 is who chose to take it.

Experiments, DAGs and what to control for · Practice #3 · Matching

Q. Match each causal-graph term to its meaning. One option on the right is not used.

1. Fork

2. Chain

3. Descendant

4. Backdoor criterion

A) Any variable reachable from a given variable by following arrows forward

B) One variable sends arrows to two others, passing non-causal association between them

C) A variable with no arrows into it or out of it

D) An adjustment set must block every backdoor path and contain nothing caused by the treatment

E) The effect flows from one variable through a middle variable to a third

Step 1 · Method

Every DAG is built from three configurations: chain \(D \to M \to Y\), fork \(D \leftarrow U \to Y\), collider \(D \to C \leftarrow Y\). Conditioning closes chains and forks and opens colliders.

Step 2 · Work

Fork: the confounder's shape, \(U\) on top. Chain: the middle variable is a mediator. Descendant: anything downstream of a node, which backdoor condition 2 forbids you to control for. Backdoor criterion: block every backdoor path, include no descendant of \(D\). The isolated node is left over; with no arrows it plays no part in identification.

Answer

1→B; 2→E; 3→A; 4→D   Fork → B, chain → E, descendant → A, backdoor criterion → D; C is not used.

Experiments, DAGs and what to control for · Practice #4 · True / false

Q. True or false: a covariate measured before treatment was assigned cannot be a collider, so controlling for pre-treatment covariates is always safe.

True

Timing rules out mediators, which the treatment must cause, but not colliders. A pre-treatment \(W\) caused by two unmeasured variables, one driving \(D\) and one driving \(Y\), is a collider.

False

Step 1 · Method

Classify a control by the graph, not by its timestamp. “Happened before the treatment?” screens out mediators; it cannot see a collider of two unmeasured causes.

Step 2 · Work

Trace the M: \(D \leftarrow U_1 \to W \leftarrow U_2 \to Y\). Left alone, the path is closed at the collider \(W\). Condition on \(W\) and it opens, so non-causal association flows from \(D\) to \(Y\). Pre-treatment is a useful screen, not a guarantee.

Answer

False   False: a pre-treatment variable can still be a collider (M-bias), and controlling for it opens a path.

SQL for economists · Topic 11

Same algebra, different order

SQL and pandas implement the same relational algebra. Clauses run FROM, WHERE, GROUP BY, HAVING, SELECT, ORDER BY: WHERE filters rows before grouping, HAVING filters groups after.

A join has a grain

Say what one row represents, before and after. A LEFT JOIN keeps every left row at least once, not exactly once: 600 customers became 4,033 rows, with nothing to warn you.

NULL is unknown

WHERE keeps only TRUE, so status != 'cancelled' also drops the NULL rows. COUNT(*) counts rows; COUNT(col) skips NULLs. Compare the two before trusting an aggregate.

Work where the data is

Parquet lets DuckDB read only the columns a query needs and skip chunks; httpfs queries a file at its URL. Filter and aggregate in SQL, then call .df() on the small result.

Q. Which SQL operation corresponds to pandas' merge()?

A) JOIN

B) SELECT DISTINCT

That is drop_duplicates(): it removes repeated rows, it does not combine tables.

C) LIMIT

That is head(): the first rows of a result.

D) UNION

UNION stacks the rows of two tables with the same columns, like pd.concat(); it does not match rows on a key.

Step 1 · Method

SQL and pandas are two grammars for the same relational algebra (Codd 1970). Translate by what the operation does to rows: filter, group, combine on a key, stack, sort, limit.

Step 2 · Work

df1.merge(df2, on='id') is FROM df1 JOIN df2 ON df1.id = df2.id. Whichever you write, check the grain: count rows before and after, because a join can multiply rows without warning.

Answer

A   A: JOIN.

Q. A 40 GB Parquet file of mortgage records sits in a public cloud bucket, and it stores the minimum and maximum of each column for every chunk of rows. On a laptop with 16 GB of memory, an analyst runs a DuckDB query that selects two of the file's forty columns and filters on one state. Which statements are true? Select all that apply.

☐ A) The query cannot run, because the file is larger than the laptop's memory

False: DuckDB streams through the file, reading only what the query needs, so file size and memory are not the limit. You do not need Spark for data bigger than RAM.

☐ B) Chunks whose min and max rule out the requested state are skipped without being read

☐ C) Only the two selected columns are read, not all forty

OLS: prediction and multivariate regression · Topics 12–13

Explain or predict

A coefficient \(\hat\beta\) or a predicted value \(\hat y\): same algebra, different rules. A post-treatment variable is a bad control for \(\hat\beta\) and fine for \(\hat y\) if it is known in time.

Judge on unseen data

In-sample \(R^2\) cannot fall when a column is added, even noise; only a test set shows overfitting. Confidence interval for a mean, prediction interval for one case.

A coefficient is a partial effect

It holds the other variables in this model fixed. OVB \(= \beta_2\delta\) exactly: two signs multiply, so you can sign the error before you see the data.

Every control is a claim

A mediator changes the question: 7,386 of a 10,133 gap ran through promotion. With an interaction the gap is \(\beta_2 + \beta_3 t\). A high VIF hurts one coefficient, not the prediction.

OLS: prediction and multivariate regression · Practice #1 · Multiple choice

Q. An analyst builds a model to forecast which small businesses will close within a year. A colleague objects that one of the strongest features, the number of late tax filings in the past quarter, is a consequence of financial distress rather than a cause of closure. Under Topic 12's two jobs for a regression, how should the feature be handled?

A) Drop it in both jobs, because a consequence of the outcome always biases a model

A post-treatment variable is a problem only when a coefficient is the deliverable. For a forecast it can be one of the best predictors.

B) Keep it in both jobs, because any variable that improves fit belongs in the model

Fit is a prediction criterion. In a causal regression a variable that improves fit can still be a bad control (Topic 10).

C) Keep it for prediction, as long as it is observed before the forecast must be made; it would be a bad control in a causal regression

D) Keep it only in a causal regression, where it blocks a backdoor path

Backwards: a consequence of distress is downstream, and controlling for it in a causal regression biases the coefficient; it does not block a backdoor path.

Step 1 · Method

Two questions: what is the effect of \(X\) on \(Y\) (\(\hat\beta\)), or what will \(Y\) be (\(\hat y\))? Same algebra; the job decides which variables belong and how success is scored.

Step 2 · Work

Late filings from the past quarter are known before the forecast is due, carry information about distress, and nobody will intervene on them, so they belong in the forecasting model. The caveat from the hospital poll: if a feature is recorded only after the forecast must be made, it is useless however predictive it looks in historical data.

Answer

C   C: keep it for prediction if it is available in time; it would be a bad control in a causal regression.

Q. Regressing hourly wage on years of schooling, statsmodels reports for 16 years of schooling a point prediction of USD 31.40, a 90% confidence interval of USD 31.05 to 31.75, and a 90% prediction interval of USD 12.60 to 50.20. A city planner wants the average wage of all workers with 16 years of schooling; a staffing agency wants to quote a wage for one specific new hire. Which interval fits each use, and how does each respond to a larger training sample?

A) Both should use the prediction interval, since it is wider and therefore safer

The planner asked about an average, which the confidence interval answers. Wider is not safer; it answers a different question.

B) The planner should use the prediction interval and the agency the confidence interval; both shrink toward zero as the sample grows

Reversed, and more data shrinks only the confidence interval; the prediction interval keeps each worker's scatter around the line.

C) Both should use the confidence interval, since it comes directly from the fitted line

Quoting 31.05 to 31.75 for a single new hire claims a precision of 70 cents for one person's wage.

D) The planner should use the confidence interval and the agency the prediction interval; only the confidence interval shrinks toward zero as the sample grows

Step 1 · Method

Two uncertainties: where the line is (confidence interval, for a mean) and where one observation lands around it (prediction interval). The PI is always wider and is the one a decision about one case needs.

Step 2 · Work

CI width 31.75 − 31.05 = 0.70; PI width 50.20 − 12.60 = 37.60, about 54 times wider. With four times the data the CI would be about half as wide; the PI would barely move, because most of its width is the scatter of individual wages.

Answer

D   D: CI for the planner, PI for the agency; only the CI shrinks toward zero.

OLS: prediction and multivariate regression · Practice #3 · Multiple choice

Q. In a simulated dataset the true effect of an extra year of schooling on annual earnings is USD 2,400. Parental income raises earnings directly, and children of richer parents complete more schooling. Regressing earnings on schooling alone returns USD 3,150; adding parental income returns USD 2,410. Which statement about omitted variable bias is correct?

A) Leaving out parental income pushed the schooling coefficient down, as an omitted variable always shrinks the effect of interest

Wrong direction and wrong rule: the sign of the bias is the product of two signs. Both are positive here, so the short regression is too big.

B) The 740 gap is an approximation that disappears only as the sample grows

In the sample, short minus long equals \(\hat\beta_2\hat\delta\) exactly, an identity; more data does not remove it, because both links remain.

C) The 740 gap equals, exactly, the effect of parental income on earnings times the slope from regressing parental income on schooling; both are positive, so the short regression overstates the return

D) Parental income cannot bias the coefficient, because it is determined before schooling

Being determined earlier is what makes parental income a confounder. Bias disappears only if \(\beta_2 = 0\) or \(\delta = 0\).

Step 1 · Method

Two conditions for bias: the omitted variable affects the outcome (\(\beta_2\)) and moves with the included predictor (\(\delta\)). The short slope lands on \(\beta_1 + \beta_2\delta\).

Step 2 · Work

Short − long = 3,150 − 2,410 = 740 = \(\hat\beta_2 \times \hat\delta\), both positive. Long 2,410 against a true 2,400 is sampling noise. The deck checked the identity on payroll data: 1,802.5 × (−1.3899) = −2,505 = short − long.

Answer

C   C: the 740 gap is exactly \(\beta_2\delta\), and two positive signs make the short regression too big.

OLS: prediction and multivariate regression · Practice #4 · Multiple choice

Q. A rent regression includes both square footage and number of rooms, which are nearly collinear in this sample; both VIFs are about 35. The standard error on square footage quadruples, the two coefficients trade size when a few rows are resampled, the coefficient on distance to transit is unchanged, and the test-set RMSE is the same to the dollar. What follows?

A) OLS is now biased, so the coefficient on distance to transit cannot be trusted either

Collinearity does not bias OLS, and the distance coefficient did not move.

B) The fitted model is still fine for predicting rents and no longer reliable for pricing one extra square foot; drop a predictor only if that coefficient is what you need

C) The sample is too small, and adding more apartments of the same kind will bring the VIFs down

More apartments of the same kind keep square footage and rooms just as correlated, so the VIFs stay near 35. Data that separates the two is what helps.

D) Any VIF above ten requires dropping the variable, whatever the purpose of the model

A VIF rule of thumb (5 or 10) matters only for a coefficient you need. For prediction, drop nothing.

Step 1 · Method

A VIF says how much a coefficient's variance is inflated because its predictor is explained by the others. Multicollinearity is about which questions the data can answer, not a data-quality problem.

Step 2 · Work

The model still knows the combined size effect, so RMSE is unchanged; it cannot split that effect between square feet and rooms. The deck's tenure twin did the same: SE on tenure 28.1 → 147.2, while \(R^2\) (0.80622) and the female coefficient (−2,746) did not move.

Answer

B   B: fine for predicting rents; too imprecise to price one extra square foot.

14 Mistakes to Avoid

1

Thinking more rows fix a structural problem: a million rows from one moment are still a cross-section.

2

Comparing money across years before putting both numbers in the same year's dollars.

3

Reading a near-zero correlation as “no relationship”: it rules out only a straight line, so plot it.

4

Believing that with enough data outliers stop mattering: one value moves the mean of a dataset of any size.

5

Reading a test's accuracy as the chance a flagged case is real: ask for the base rate.

6

Trusting a huge self-selected sample over a small random one: more data shrinks only the “missed” term.

7

Thinking the bootstrap creates new data: it measures variance and inherits every bias in the sample.

8

Reading a p-value as the probability the null is true.

9

Thinking added controls fix selection bias: randomisation balances what you did not measure; controls do not.

10

Controlling for a mediator or a collider because it improves the fit.

11

Assuming a LEFT JOIN keeps the left table's row count, or that != keeps the NULL rows in SQL.

12

Handing over the confidence interval when the decision is about one case: that needs the prediction interval.

13

Assuming an omitted variable always pushes the estimate up: the sign of the bias is the product of two signs.

14

Reading the female coefficient in an interaction model as the average gap: it is the gap at tenure zero.
